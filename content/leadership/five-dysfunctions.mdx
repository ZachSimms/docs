Patrick Lencioni's five dysfunctions model, from *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team: A Leadership Fable* (2002):
what each dysfunction looks like, how to diagnose it, the leader's job and the book's exercises for fixing it,
plus the evidence-based team research that complements it (psychological safety, Project Aristotle, Tuckman,
Hackman). The team leader's personal stance is in [extreme ownership](/leadership/extreme-ownership/), intent and
delegation in [mission command](/leadership/mission-command/), one-on-ones, feedback and hiring in
[people management](/leadership/management/), and how teams decide in
[decision-making models](/thinking/mental-models/decision-making/).

## The model at a glance

The book is a novel about Kathryn Petersen, the new CEO of a fictional start-up, DecisionTech, fixing an executive
team that is smart and well paid but not working as a team. The last part of the book sets out the model directly.

| # | Dysfunction | Shows up as | Healthy opposite: members… | Leader's main job |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | **Absence of trust** | invulnerability | admit weaknesses, mistakes and requests for help | go first on vulnerability; don't punish it |
| 2 | **Fear of conflict** | artificial harmony | debate ideas openly and passionately | hold back from rescuing people from productive conflict; model it |
| 3 | **Lack of commitment** | ambiguity | commit to clear decisions, even after disagreeing | push for closure and deadlines; accept that some decisions will be wrong |
| 4 | **Avoidance of accountability** | low standards | call out peers' performance and behavior | let peers hold each other to account; be the final backstop |
| 5 | **Inattention to results** | status and ego | put collective results above individual or departmental ones | set a results tone; reward team outcomes, not personal ones |

### Why a pyramid

Lencioni's argument is a causal chain, read from the bottom:

- Without **trust**, people won't risk open disagreement, so conflict goes underground.
- Without real **conflict**, people haven't aired their views, so they don't truly buy in to decisions.
- Without **commitment**, nobody has signed up to a clear plan, so nobody feels entitled to hold a peer to it.
- Without **accountability**, standards slip and people drift to what earns them personal status.
- The top dysfunction is the one that actually costs you: the team stops **focusing on results**.

The practical conclusion: you can't fix accountability with a new dashboard if people don't trust each other
enough to argue. Work from the base upward, while keeping the results at the top in view.

<Callout type="warn" title="The ordering is an argument, not a finding">
  The pyramid's sequence comes from Lencioni's consulting experience and the logic of the fable. It has not been
  validated as a causal ordering by peer-reviewed research. Treat it as a useful diagnostic checklist, not a law.
  See [criticisms](#criticisms-and-evidence-quality).
</Callout>

## Trust: vulnerability-based vs predictive

Lencioni defines team trust as confidence among members that their peers' intentions are good, so there is no
reason to be protective or careful around the group (paraphrased). He distinguishes it from the everyday kind.

| | Predictive trust | Vulnerability-based trust |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Meaning | I can predict how you'll behave, from experience | I can show you my weaknesses and you won't use them against me |
| Built by | time and a track record | leaders and members going first on admissions |
| Sounds like | "She always ships on Friday" | "I got this wrong", "I need help", "You're better at this than me" |
| Enough for | reliable hand-offs | honest debate, fast learning, admitting mistakes early |
| Failure mode | people trust you'll behave the way you always do, including defensively | forced, performative disclosure that feels unsafe |

A team can have lots of predictive trust (everyone knows who will be defensive) and none of the vulnerability kind.

## 1. Absence of trust

**Shows up as invulnerability**: people hide mistakes and weaknesses, don't ask for help, and assume bad intent.

| Looks like | Healthy team |
| :--- | :--- |
| concealing mistakes until they can't be hidden | admits mistakes early, in the meeting, unprompted |
| no requests for help outside one's own area | asks for and offers help freely |
| jumping to conclusions about others' motives | assumes good intent, asks before reacting |
| holding grudges, dreading meetings | looks forward to meetings as useful |
| performing competence in status updates | status updates include "I'm stuck on…" |
| wasted energy managing impressions | energy goes on the work |

**Diagnostic questions**

- When did someone on this team last say "I was wrong" in front of the others? Who was it?
- Does anyone ask a peer (not the boss) for help with something in their own area?
- Do problems surface in the meeting, or in the corridor and in DMs after it?
- If a teammate missed a deadline, would the first assumption be incompetence, laziness, or a reason we don't know?

**Leader's role**: demonstrate vulnerability first, genuinely (not staged), and create an environment that does not
punish it. If the leader never admits a mistake, nobody else will.

| Exercise (from the book) | What it is | Time |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Personal histories exercise** | each person answers a few low-risk questions about their background: hometown, number of siblings, an interesting childhood challenge, first job | under an hour |
| **Team effectiveness exercise** | each person names the single biggest contribution and the one area to improve for each teammate, going round the table one person at a time | about an hour; needs some trust first |
| **Personality and behavioral preference profiles** | a profiling tool (Lencioni favors the Myers–Briggs Type Indicator) so people understand and stop misreading each other's styles | half a day with a facilitator |
| **360-degree feedback** | peers rate each other; Lencioni advises keeping it separate from pay and formal appraisal | varies |
| **Experiential team exercises** | ropes courses and similar; Lencioni treats them as useful only if tied to real work | a day |

<Note kind="info">The MBTI is widely used but psychometrically criticized (types are not bimodal and many people change type on retest). Any profile works for this exercise if it gets people talking about differences; don't use it for selection.</Note>

## 2. Fear of conflict

**Shows up as artificial harmony**: meetings are polite and dull, real disagreements are voiced only afterward, and
important topics are avoided. Lencioni means **productive ideological conflict** about concepts and ideas, not
personal attacks. He places it on a continuum from artificial harmony at one end to mean-spirited personal attacks
at the other, with the ideal point near the middle; most teams sit too far toward harmony.

| Looks like | Healthy team |
| :--- | :--- |
| boring meetings; agreement too fast | lively meetings; ideas get stress-tested |
| back-channel politics and personal attacks after the meeting | disagreement happens in the room, about the idea |
| controversial topics never reach the agenda | the hardest topic goes first |
| silence read as agreement | silence treated as a missing view and drawn out |
| issues resurface again and again | issues get resolved and stay resolved |

**Diagnostic questions**

- When did the team last change its mind in a meeting because of an argument?
- What's the topic everyone knows about but nobody raises?
- Are the real decisions made in the meeting, or in one-to-ones and hallways afterward?
- After a meeting, do people say to each other what they didn't say during it?

**Leader's role**: show restraint when people engage in conflict, and let resolution happen naturally rather than
stepping in early to protect people. Model healthy conflict yourself: disagree with a peer openly and respectfully.

| Exercise | What it is |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Mining** | one person takes the role of "miner of conflict": they dig out buried disagreements and force the team to work through them |
| **Real-time permission** | during a debate, when people start to look uncomfortable, someone interrupts to remind them that this conflict is productive and they should keep going |
| **Thomas–Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument** | a questionnaire (TKI) that shows each member's default conflict style: competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding, accommodating |
| **Conflict norms** | the team writes down what acceptable conflict looks like (see the template below) |

```text
CONFLICT NORMS (agree as a team, revisit quarterly)
1. We argue about ideas, never about people's motives.
2. Silence is not agreement. The chair asks each quiet
   person for their view before a decision.
3. Bad news goes first on the agenda.
4. "I disagree because…" is always welcome; sarcasm isn't.
5. Anyone can call "real-time permission" when a debate
   gets uncomfortable but is still useful.
6. We don't relitigate a decision in hallways. Reopen it
   only in the room, with new information.
7. Heat is fine; contempt, eye-rolling and interruptions
   aren't. Anyone can call it out.
```

## 3. Lack of commitment

**Shows up as ambiguity**: decisions are vague, people leave meetings with different understandings, and work
stalls while people wait for more data or for consensus. Lencioni names two causes: the desire for **consensus**
and the need for **certainty**. His point is that reasonable people don't need their way to buy in; they need to
have been heard and to know their view was considered. Amazon's version of the same idea is "disagree and commit"
(Bezos, 2016 shareholder letter).

| Looks like | Healthy team |
| :--- | :--- |
| direction and priorities unclear | clarity on direction and priorities |
| windows of opportunity missed through over-analysis | moves ahead without hesitation |
| same decisions discussed again and again | decisions stay made |
| second-guessing after the meeting | aligned behind decisions, including those who argued against |
| people waiting for certainty | willing to change course if the decision turns out wrong |

**Diagnostic questions**

- At the end of the last meeting, could everyone write down the same three decisions?
- What did different team members tell their own reports about that meeting?
- How many decisions from last month were reopened without new information?
- Who has the final call when the team can't agree, and does everyone know it?

**Leader's role**: be comfortable making a decision that ultimately turns out to be wrong, keep pushing for closure,
and hold the team to the schedules it sets. Don't let the team hold out for certainty or unanimity.

| Exercise | What it is |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Cascading messaging** (cascading communication) | at the end of a meeting, the team explicitly reviews the key decisions and agrees what each member will tell their people, and what stays confidential |
| **Deadlines** | clear dates for decisions, including interim milestones; ambiguity is the enemy |
| **Contingency and worst-case scenario analysis** | spell out the worst realistic outcome and a fallback; it's usually less bad than feared, so the team can commit |
| **Low-risk exposure therapy** | practice decisiveness on low-stakes decisions to show the team that fast decisions made after discussion rarely cause harm |

```text
CASCADING COMMUNICATION (last 10 minutes of every meeting)
Decision 1: ______________________________________
  Owner: ________  By: ________
  What we tell our teams (same words): ____________
  Confidential until: ____________
Decision 2: ...
Check: does anyone's version differ? Fix it now.
Send: each member tells their team within 24 hours.
```

## 4. Avoidance of accountability

**Shows up as low standards**: people won't call peers on behavior or performance that hurts the team, so
mediocrity spreads and the leader becomes the only source of discipline. Lencioni means **peer-to-peer**
accountability, which he argues is more effective than accountability that runs only through the boss. It depends on
commitment: you can't hold someone to a plan they never signed up to.

| Looks like | Healthy team |
| :--- | :--- |
| resentment between people with different standards | poor performers feel pressure from peers to improve |
| mediocrity encouraged by silence | problems identified quickly, by peers |
| missed deadlines and key deliverables | respect between members held to the same high standard |
| the leader as the sole source of discipline | less bureaucracy around performance management |

**Diagnostic questions**

- When a peer's work slips, who says something first: a peer, the manager, or nobody?
- Are the team's goals and each person's commitments written down where everyone can see them?
- Is there anyone whose behavior everyone complains about privately and nobody addresses?
- Have standards drifted downward over the last six months without anyone deciding they should?

**Leader's role**: encourage and allow the team to be the first and primary accountability mechanism. When the team
fails to act, the leader must be willing to be the ultimate arbiter of discipline, which is what keeps peer
accountability credible.

| Exercise | What it is |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Publication of goals and standards** | write down and make public what the team has committed to achieve, who owns what, and the behaviors expected |
| **Simple and regular progress reviews** | short, structured reviews (verbal or written) where members report against commitments and give each other feedback |
| **Team rewards** | shift some rewards from individual to team performance, so peers have a reason to hold each other to account |

```text
TEAM COMMITMENTS BOARD (visible to everyone, reviewed weekly)
| Commitment              | Owner | Due    | Status | Note |
| Launch pricing page v2  | Ana   | 12 Oct | amber  | ...  |
| Hire 1 backend engineer | Raj   | 31 Oct | green  | ...  |
| Cut p95 latency < 300ms | Wei   | 30 Nov | red    | ...  |

Behavior standards: we reply to blocking questions the
same day; we tell a peer directly before escalating; we
flag slips as soon as we know, not on the due date.
```

## 5. Inattention to results

**Shows up as status and ego**: members care more about their own career, department or visibility than about the
team's collective goals. Lencioni singles out **team status** (just being on the team is enough) and **individual
status** (looking good personally) as the two distractions.

| Looks like | Healthy team |
| :--- | :--- |
| stagnation; the team fails to grow | results-oriented people are retained |
| rarely beats competitors | individualistic behavior is minimized |
| achievement-oriented people leave | the team enjoys success and suffers failure together |
| members focus on their careers and departments | members subordinate their own goals to the team's |
| easily distracted by side projects | avoids distractions |

**Diagnostic questions**

- Can every member name the team's top three results for this quarter, and the current score?
- If the team hit its goals but your department missed one of its own, would you count it a good quarter?
- Do people argue harder for their own budget and headcount than for the shared goal?
- What gets celebrated: individual wins or team wins?

**Leader's role**: set the tone that results come first, be selfless and objective, and reserve rewards and
recognition for people who make real contributions to the group's goals.

| Exercise | What it is |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Public declaration of results** | commit publicly to specific results; a team that says out loud what it will achieve works harder to hit it |
| **Results-based rewards** | tie rewards to achieving specific team outcomes rather than effort or tenure |
| **A team scoreboard** (general practice, not specific to the book) | 3–6 shared measures reviewed at every meeting, so results are always in view |

## The first team

Lencioni's later book *The Advantage* (2012) names an idea already present in the fable: for every member of a
leadership team, **that leadership team is their "first team"**, with priority over the department they run. A VP of
engineering is first a member of the executive team and only second the head of engineering.

| Without a first team | With a first team |
| :--- | :--- |
| staff meetings are status reports from department heads defending turf | staff meetings are where cross-functional trade-offs get argued out |
| each leader optimizes their own function | leaders give up budget or people when the company needs it |
| conflicts between departments escalate to the CEO | peers resolve conflicts between their own departments |
| managers are loyal downward, to their reports | managers are loyal sideways, to peers, and model that to their reports |

For a small start-up, the "leadership team" may be the founders plus two or three leads. The same rule applies: the
founding group's shared goals come before any one person's area.

## Team self-assessment

A self-scoring checklist in the spirit of the book's 15-item assessment (paraphrased and extended, not the book's
wording). Each member scores each statement independently: **3 = usually, 2 = sometimes, 1 = rarely**. Add the three
scores for each dysfunction.

```text
TRUST
 [ ] T1 People admit their mistakes and weaknesses to the team.
 [ ] T2 People ask teammates for help without worrying
        how it looks.
 [ ] T3 People know about each other's lives and work
        and use it to understand each other.
CONFLICT
 [ ] C1 Discussion of important issues is passionate and
        unguarded.
 [ ] C2 Meetings tackle the most important and difficult
        topics rather than the easy ones.
 [ ] C3 People voice their real opinions even when they
        risk disagreeing with the leader.
COMMITMENT
 [ ] M1 Meetings end with clear, specific decisions that
        everyone can repeat.
 [ ] M2 People support decisions they argued against.
 [ ] M3 Decisions are not reopened without new information.
ACCOUNTABILITY
 [ ] A1 People point out unproductive behavior in peers.
 [ ] A2 Peers, not only the leader, raise performance
        slips.
 [ ] A3 People apologize to one another and hold each other
        to what was agreed.
RESULTS
 [ ] R1 Morale is hit by missing team goals, not only
        personal ones.
 [ ] R2 People give up credit and resources for the team's
        results.
 [ ] R3 People put team goals ahead of their department's.

SCORING (per dysfunction, 3 statements, 3-9 points)
 8-9  probably not a problem
 6-7  could become a problem
 3-5  probably needs addressing now
Compare individual scores: a wide spread on one area is
itself a finding.
```

The book's scoring bands are the same as those above. Lencioni's
publisher also sells a commercial assessment, *The Five Behaviors of a Cohesive Team* (Wiley), built on the model.

## Worked scenarios

<details>
<summary>Scenario 1: the silent standup (trust and conflict)</summary>

**Situation.** A six-person start-up engineering team. Standups are fine; sprint goals are missed two sprints in
three. In one-to-ones, two engineers say the architecture chosen by the tech lead is wrong, but neither said so when
it was proposed.

**Diagnosis.** Artificial harmony (dysfunction 2) sitting on low trust (dysfunction 1): the tech lead is also the
founder's first hire, and disagreeing feels like a political risk.

**Actions.**

1. The founder opens the next retro by naming one of their own recent bad calls and what it cost (vulnerability first).
2. Run the personal histories exercise at the start of the next planning session (30 minutes).
3. Agree conflict norms; the tech lead volunteers to be "mined" first: "What's the strongest case against our design?"
4. The founder practises restraint: when the debate gets heated, they say "this is useful, keep going" instead of
   ruling.

**Result to look for.** Within a month, design objections come up in design review, not in one-to-ones.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Scenario 2: the decision that won't stay made (commitment)</summary>

**Situation.** Leadership chose to drop a legacy product line in March. By May it's been "revisited" in three
meetings; sales still quotes it; support doesn't know what to tell customers.

**Diagnosis.** Lack of commitment. The decision was made without the head of sales fully voicing objections, and
there was no cascading communication, so each function told its teams something different.

**Actions.**

1. Reopen once, properly: the head of sales presents the strongest case to keep it; the team debates; the CEO decides.
2. Worst-case analysis: "If we're wrong, we lose about 8% of renewals; the fallback is a paid extended-support plan."
3. Cascading communication: the same three sentences go to every team within 24 hours.
4. Deadline: last quote date 30 June, published.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Scenario 3: the star who doesn't play for the team (accountability and results)</summary>

**Situation.** A senior engineer ships the most code but ignores code review requests, skips on-call hand-offs and
lets juniors flounder. Peers complain to the manager, not to him.

**Diagnosis.** Avoidance of accountability (peers won't raise it) feeding inattention to results (his individual
output is valued over team throughput).

**Actions.**

1. Publish team standards: review turnaround within one working day, on-call hand-off notes required.
2. Change what's measured: team cycle time and incident count on the scoreboard, not individual commits.
3. Coach peers to give him direct feedback first (see [feedback models](/leadership/management/#feedback)).
4. If peers have raised it and nothing changes, the manager acts as the final arbiter: a clear expectations
   conversation with consequences.

</details>

## What the evidence says: complementary models

Lencioni's model is practitioner wisdom. These models come from research and mostly point the same way.

### Psychological safety (Edmondson)

Amy Edmondson defined **team psychological safety** as "a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is
safe for interpersonal risk taking" (Edmondson 1999, *Administrative Science Quarterly*). In a study of 51 work teams
in a manufacturing company, psychological safety was associated with team learning behavior, which in turn was
associated with performance. *The Fearless Organization* (2018) is the practitioner book.

- It's close to Lencioni's vulnerability-based trust, but defined at the team level and measured with a validated
  survey.
- A meta-analysis of 136 samples (Frazier et al. 2017, *Personnel Psychology*) linked it to task performance and
  organisational citizenship behavior, among other outcomes.
- Edmondson stresses that safety isn't the same as niceness or low standards. She crosses safety with performance
  standards to get four zones:

| | Low standards | High standards |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **High psychological safety** | comfort zone | **learning zone** |
| **Low psychological safety** | apathy zone | anxiety zone |

### Google's Project Aristotle

Google People Operations studied what made its own teams effective: over two years, 200+ interviews, 250+
attributes, 180+ teams (Julia Rozovsky, re:Work blog, 17 November 2015). Who was on the team mattered less than how
members interacted, structured their work and viewed their contributions. Five dynamics:

| Dynamic | The question it asks |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Psychological safety** | can we take risks on this team without feeling insecure or embarrassed? |
| **Dependability** | can we count on each other to do high-quality work on time? |
| **Structure and clarity** | are goals, roles and execution plans clear? |
| **Meaning** | are we working on something personally important to each of us? |
| **Impact** | do we believe the work we're doing matters? |

Psychological safety was by far the most important, the base for the other four. Evidence quality: an internal
study, not peer reviewed, with no published data. It is consistent with the academic literature, but don't cite it
as if it were a controlled experiment.

### Tuckman's stages

Bruce Tuckman (1965, *Psychological Bulletin*) reviewed studies of small groups, mostly therapy and training groups,
and proposed **forming → storming → norming → performing**. Tuckman and Mary Ann Jensen added **adjourning** in 1977.

| Stage | What happens | Leader's move |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| forming | polite, uncertain, dependent on the leader | clear purpose, roles and ground rules |
| storming | conflict over roles, direction and power | surface conflict and keep it about ideas (Lencioni's dysfunction 2) |
| norming | agreed ways of working; cohesion | write the norms down; start delegating |
| performing | self-managing, focused on the task | get out of the way; stretch goals |
| adjourning | the team disbands or changes | close properly; capture lessons; recognize people |

Caveats: it's a descriptive model, not tested as a fixed sequence; teams can loop back (a new member can restart
storming). Connie Gersick's study of project teams (1988, *Academy of Management Journal*) found a different
pattern: inertia until roughly the midpoint of the deadline, then a burst of change. That suggests deadlines matter as
much as stages.

### Hackman's conditions for team effectiveness

J. Richard Hackman (*Leading Teams*, 2002) argued that a leader can't make a team great; they can only create
conditions that make greatness more likely.

| Condition | Meaning | Test question |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Real team** | bounded (clear who's in), interdependent, stable over time | does everyone agree who is on the team? |
| **Compelling direction** | a clear, challenging, consequential purpose | could each member state it in one sentence? |
| **Enabling structure** | a well-designed task, the right size and mix, clear norms of conduct | is the team small enough, and are norms explicit? |
| **Supportive context** | rewards, information, training and resources from the wider organization | does the company reward team outcomes, and give the team the data it needs? |
| **Expert coaching** | help at the right moments (launch, midpoint, end) | is anyone coaching the team as a team? |

- In the 2009 HBR interview "Why Teams Don't Work", Hackman reported that across more than 120 senior leadership
  teams, fewer than 10% of members agreed about who was on their own team.
- He also cited evidence that **newness is a liability**: an NTSB finding that 73% of incidents in its database
  happened on a crew's first day flying together.
- Hackman and Ruth Wageman's rough **60–30–10 rule**: about 60% of the difference in team performance comes from
  how the team was designed, 30% from how it was launched, and 10% from coaching once work is under way. It's a
  rule of thumb from their research, not a precise estimate.

### Team size

Hackman: "My rule of thumb is no double digits" (HBR 2009); he capped student teams at six. Links between members grow
quadratically:

$$
\text{links} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2} \qquad n=5 \Rightarrow 10,\quad n=10 \Rightarrow 45,\quad n=15 \Rightarrow 105
$$

Amazon's **two-pizza team** rule, attributed to Jeff Bezos, says the same thing: a team should be small enough to
feed with two pizzas. The exact number matters less than keeping teams small enough for everyone to know what everyone
else is doing.

## Lencioni vs Edmondson vs Aristotle vs Hackman

| | Lencioni (2002) | Edmondson (1999, 2018) | Project Aristotle (2015) | Hackman (2002) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Type | business fable plus model | academic construct, field studies | internal company analysis | decades of academic research |
| Unit | leadership team | work team | Google teams | any work team |
| Core claim | trust → conflict → commitment → accountability → results | psychological safety enables learning, which drives performance | psychological safety underpins dependability, clarity, meaning, impact | design conditions matter more than day-to-day leadership |
| Leader's lever | behavior in the room: vulnerability, restraint, closure | framing work as learning, inviting input, responding well to bad news | norms and rituals around the five dynamics | who's on the team, the direction, the structure, the context |
| Evidence | anecdotal; no validated causal order | strong, replicated, meta-analyzed | suggestive; unpublished data | substantial, mainly observational |
| Blind spot | team design, size, skills, external context | says less about accountability and decisions | internal to one company | less about interpersonal tactics |
| Best use | diagnosing and running a leadership off-site | measuring and building safety | a simple team health check | setting up or restructuring a team |

The models fit together: Hackman tells you how to **set up** a team, Edmondson and Aristotle what **climate** it
needs, Lencioni what **behaviors** to look for and practice inside it.

## Criticisms and evidence quality

| Criticism | Detail |
| :--- | :--- |
| **fable format** | the book is a novel; the story is constructed to illustrate the model, so it can't test it |
| **no empirical validation of the ordering** | nothing shows that trust must come before conflict, or commitment before accountability. The dysfunctions likely reinforce each other in loops rather than stacking neatly. |
| **not built on research** | Gordon Curphy and Robert Hogan (*The Rocket Model*, 2012) note that the book is explicitly fiction, not based on research, and its recommendations lack empirical support. They argue trust grows from working through real tasks together, not personal-disclosure exercises. |
| **ignores team design** | nothing on team size, skills, clarity of purpose or resources, which Hackman's research finds matter most |
| **leadership-team bias** | written for executive teams; adapts reasonably well to small product teams, less well to large or distributed ones |
| **tools with weak psychometrics** | the MBTI, which Lencioni recommends, is criticized by psychologists for low reliability and forced types |
| **vulnerability can be coerced** | forced disclosure exercises can feel unsafe, especially for people from under-represented groups; make them opt-in and low-risk |

Plainly: the five dysfunctions are a memorable, practically useful checklist that lines up well with psychological
safety research, but the pyramid is not an empirically validated model. Use it to start conversations and structure
interventions; use Edmondson's survey or Aristotle's five questions if you want to measure.

## A 90-day plan to fix a dysfunctional team

<Steps>
<Step title="Days 1–14: diagnose">

- One-to-one with every member: what's working, what's not, what they'd change, what they haven't said in meetings.
- Everyone completes the self-assessment independently; you tabulate averages and spread per dysfunction.
- Check Hackman's conditions: is the team real, small enough, with a compelling direction and the resources it needs?
  Fix structural problems first; no exercise will overcome a badly designed team.
- Write one page: the top two dysfunctions, with evidence.

</Step>
<Step title="Days 15–30: launch or relaunch (trust)">

- A half-day or full-day off-site. Share the assessment results openly.
- Leader goes first: a real mistake, a real weakness, a request for help.
- Personal histories exercise; if trust allows, the team effectiveness exercise.
- Agree conflict norms and a short list of behavior standards.

</Step>
<Step title="Days 31–60: conflict and commitment">

- In each weekly meeting, put the hardest topic first. Assign a rotating "miner of conflict".
- Use real-time permission whenever a debate gets uncomfortable but useful.
- End every meeting with cascading communication: decisions, owners, dates, what we tell our teams.
- Use deadlines and worst-case analysis to close decisions that have been hanging.

</Step>
<Step title="Days 61–90: accountability and results">

- Publish team goals, owners and standards where everyone can see them.
- Weekly progress review against commitments; peers give feedback before the leader does.
- A team scoreboard of 3–6 results, reviewed at every meeting.
- Move some recognition (and, where you can, rewards) to team outcomes.
- Deal with anyone who persistently undermines the team; a team watches how the leader handles its worst behavior.

</Step>
<Step title="Day 90: re-measure">

- Rerun the self-assessment and compare. Look for movement in the lowest area and a narrower spread.
- Keep what worked as permanent rituals; drop what felt like theatre.
- Schedule a quarterly check-in to repeat the assessment.

</Step>
</Steps>

```text
QUARTERLY TEAM HEALTH CHECK (30 minutes)
1. Each person scores the five Aristotle questions 1-5:
   safety, dependability, clarity, meaning, impact.
2. Share scores simultaneously (avoid anchoring).
3. Discuss the lowest average and the widest spread.
4. Agree one norm or ritual to change. Owner + date.
5. Review last quarter's change: keep, adjust or drop.
```

### Common mistakes when fixing a team

- Running trust exercises while ignoring a structural problem (unclear goals, wrong people, too big a team).
- Leader asks for vulnerability but never shows it, or reacts badly the first time someone admits a mistake.
- Treating conflict norms as a poster rather than enforcing them in the next heated meeting.
- Confusing consensus with commitment, and waiting for everyone to agree before deciding.
- Leaving accountability entirely to the leader, which teaches peers to complain upward instead of speaking directly.
- Measuring individual output only, then wondering why nobody attends to team results.
- Declaring victory after the off-site. Behavior change shows in the next 20 meetings, not the first.
