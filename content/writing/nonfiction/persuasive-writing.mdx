## Rhetoric basics

Aristotle, *Rhetoric* (4th century BC, trans. W. Rhys Roberts): "Of the modes of persuasion furnished by the
spoken word there are three kinds. The first kind depends on the personal character of the speaker; the second
on putting the audience into a certain frame of mind; the third on the proof, or apparent proof, provided by the
words of the speech itself."

| Appeal | Greek sense | On the page | Fails when |
| :----- | :---------- | :---------- | :--------- |
| **Ethos** | character, credibility | relevant track record, candour about weaknesses, accurate details, a named author who owns the recommendation | credentials are irrelevant or inflated; one wrong number destroys it |
| **Pathos** | the audience's state of mind | the reader's stakes, a concrete customer story, vivid consequences | it replaces evidence, or the emotion is manufactured |
| **Logos** | the argument itself | a clear claim, reasons, data, worked examples, the alternatives and why they lose | the logic is sound but the reader doesn't care (no pathos) or doesn't trust you (no ethos) |
| **Kairos** | the opportune moment | why now: a deadline, a market shift, a budget cycle, a recent incident | the same proposal lands differently a quarter earlier or later |

Aristotle adds that credibility "should be achieved by what the speaker says, not by what people think of his
character before he begins to speak". On the page, ethos is built by precision: correct numbers, fair statement
of the other side, and the specific detail only someone who has done the work would know.

## Know the reader's decision

Persuasive writing starts from the reader's decision, not your content. Before drafting, answer these.

| Question | Why it matters |
| :------- | :------------- |
| Who decides? | a document aimed at "the team" persuades nobody; name the decision-maker |
| What exactly are they deciding? | "approve \$40k for a pilot" is decidable; "think about AI" is not |
| What do they care about? | revenue, risk, their team's time, their boss's goals, their reputation |
| What do they already believe? | start from shared ground; don't argue what they already accept |
| What will make them say no? | the objections you must answer in the document |
| What is the cost of saying yes? | money, time, reversibility, political capital |
| What happens if they do nothing? | the real alternative is usually the status quo, not a competitor |
| When do they need to decide? | kairos; and your deadline, if you have one |

Two rules follow:

- **One document, one ask.** Two asks halve the chance of either. Split them.
- **Make the ask the easy path.** Reduce the cost of saying yes: a pilot instead of a rollout, a reversible
  decision, a default that happens unless they object.

## Answer first: the Pyramid Principle

Barbara Minto developed the Pyramid Principle at McKinsey in the 1960s–70s and published it as a book in the
1980s (her site dates the original book to 1987; the expanded *The Minto Pyramid Principle* appeared in 1996).
Her summary: "your thinking will be easy for a reader to grasp if you present the ideas organized as a pyramid
under a single point."

| Rule | Meaning |
| :--- | :------ |
| **Answer first** | the governing thought (recommendation, conclusion) goes at the top |
| **Summarise below** | each idea summarises the group of ideas beneath it |
| **Group logically** | ideas in a group are the same kind of idea and are MECE (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive, Minto's term) |
| **Order logically** | within a group: deductive (argument chain), chronological, structural or by importance |
| **Vertical Q&A** | each statement raises a question in the reader's mind ("why?", "how?"); the level below answers it |

```text
            Recommendation: move billing to Stripe by Q1
          /                     |                       \
 Cuts cost 30%         Removes PCI scope        Unblocks EU launch
 - fees vs current     - no card data stored    - SEPA, VAT built in
 - fewer FTE hours     - audit shrinks          - 6 weeks vs 6 months
```

### SCQ and SCQA introductions

Minto's introduction structure (her "SCQ Framework") tells a story the reader already agrees with, then raises
the question the document answers.

| Part | Content | Example |
| :--- | :------ | :------ |
| **Situation** | uncontroversial context the reader accepts | "We process 40,000 payments a month through our in-house billing system." |
| **Complication** | what changed or went wrong; creates tension | "The EU launch needs SEPA and VAT handling, which would take six months to build, and our PCI audit costs \$120k a year." |
| **Question** | the question the complication raises (often implicit) | "Should we keep building billing ourselves?" |
| **Answer** | the governing thought (top of the pyramid) | "No: move to Stripe by Q1." |

**SCQA** is the common name for the four-part version. For an impatient reader, reorder to **A-SC**: answer
first, then the situation and complication that justify it.

### The "so what?" test

For every statement, ask "so what?" until the answer is something the reader cares about. Consulting practice
calls the result an **insight** as opposed to an **observation**.

| Observation | So what? | Insight (write this) |
| :---------- | :------- | :------------------- |
| "Churn rose to 4% in Q3." | concentrated in accounts on the old plan | "Customers on the legacy plan are leaving: migrating them would cut churn by half." |
| "The API has 120 endpoints." | 30 are unused | "Deleting 30 unused endpoints halves our security review." |
| "Competitor X raised \$50M." | they will cut prices | "Expect a price war in the mid-market by spring; lock in annual contracts now." |

## Memos and narratives

### The Amazon six-pager

Jeff Bezos's email to Amazon's senior team on 9 June 2004 had the subject line "No powerpoint presentations from
now on at steam". Its reasoning (as published by Business Insider in 2015):

> The reason writing a 4 page memo is harder than "writing" a 20 page powerpoint is because the narrative
> structure of a good memo forces better thought and better understanding of what's more important than what,
> and how things are related.

In the 2017 shareholder letter: "We don't do PowerPoint (or any other slide-oriented) presentations at Amazon.
Instead, we write narratively structured six-page memos. We silently read one at the beginning of each meeting
in a kind of 'study hall.'" And on quality: "The great memos are written and re-written, shared with colleagues
who are asked to improve the work, set aside for a couple of days, and then edited again with a fresh mind."

| Practice | Why |
| :------- | :-- |
| full sentences, no bullet lists for reasoning | forces the logical connections bullets let you skip |
| silent reading at the start of the meeting | everyone has actually read it; no presenter charisma |
| appendices for data | the narrative stays at six pages; the evidence is available |
| comments in the doc, then discussion | the meeting is spent on disagreement, not comprehension |
| multiple drafts over days | quality comes from rewriting and outside review |

### PR/FAQ (working backwards)

Colin Bryar and Bill Carr, *Working Backwards* (2021), describe Amazon's PR/FAQ: write the launch **press
release** and the **FAQs** before building anything, to force the author to start from the customer. Their
published template's components:

| Part | Content |
| :--- | :------ |
| Heading | name the product so the target customer understands it, one sentence |
| Subheading | who the customer is and what benefit they get, one sentence |
| Summary paragraph | city, outlet, planned launch date; the product and its benefits |
| Problem paragraph | the customer's problem, from the customer's point of view |
| Solution paragraph(s) | how the product solves it, and how it beats what customers use today |
| Quotes and getting started | a company spokesperson quote, a hypothetical customer quote, how to start |
| External FAQ | questions customers and press will ask (price, how it works, returns) |
| Internal FAQ | questions leadership will ask: market size, cost, risks, dependencies, what must be true |

The press release is short (about a page); the FAQ carries the hard thinking. Bryar and Carr describe the review
as 15–20 minutes of silent reading followed by discussion aimed at truth-seeking, not selling.

## Proposals and decision docs

### One-page proposal

A one-pager fits when the decision is modest and the reader is senior. The constraint is the point: if it does
not fit on a page, you have not decided what matters.

| Section | Length |
| :------ | :----- |
| Ask (BLUF) | 1–2 sentences: what, how much, by when |
| Problem | 2–4 sentences with one number |
| Proposal | what you will do; scope and non-scope |
| Why this, why now | the alternatives briefly; the timing argument |
| Cost and risk | money, people, time; what could go wrong; how reversible |
| Success measure | the metric, target and check-in date |

### Decision doc

For contested or high-stakes decisions, lay out options fairly. A reader who spots a straw-man option stops
trusting the whole document.

| Section | Content |
| :------ | :------ |
| Decision needed | the question, the decider, the deadline |
| Context | facts only, value-neutral; what constraints are fixed |
| Options | 2–4 real options, including "do nothing" |
| Comparison | a table of options against the criteria that matter |
| Recommendation | which option and why, stated plainly |
| Risks and mitigations | for the recommended option; what would change your mind |
| Reversibility | one-way or two-way door; cost to undo |

| Criterion | A: build in-house | B: Stripe | C: do nothing |
| :-------- | :---------------- | :-------- | :------------ |
| time to EU launch | 6 months | 6 weeks | blocked |
| annual cost | \$300k eng time | 0.4% of volume | \$120k audit |
| control of UX | full | high | full |
| reversibility | high | medium (migration) | n/a |

## Emails that get answered

The reader decides in the preview pane whether to act now, later or never. Write for that pane.

| Element | Rule | Before | After |
| :------ | :--- | :----- | :---- |
| Subject | state the action and deadline | "Quick question" | "Approve Q4 contractor budget by Thu?" |
| First line | BLUF: the ask or the news | "Hope you're well! I wanted to reach out regarding…" | "Can you approve \$18k for two contractors in Q4?" |
| Body | only what the reader needs to decide | a history of the project | three lines: why, what, what if not |
| One ask | one question per email | four questions in one paragraph | one question; other topics in other emails |
| Deadline | a real date and the reason | "when you get a chance" | "by Thursday, so they can start on the 1st" |
| Easy yes | make replying trivial | "Thoughts?" | "Reply 'yes' and I'll send the contracts." |
| Length | fits on a phone screen | 400 words | under 150 words |

**Full example.**

```text
BEFORE
Subject: Contractors

Hi Sam,

Hope you had a great weekend! I wanted to reach out about
the Q4 plan. As you know we've been discussing the mobile
app timeline for a while, and there have been some
challenges with hiring, which I think we talked about in
the last planning meeting. Anyway, the team has been
looking at options and we think it might make sense to
consider bringing in some contractors, potentially two,
though we could discuss. Budget would be roughly $18k I
think. Let me know your thoughts when you get a chance!

AFTER
Subject: Approve $18k for 2 contractors by Thu?

Hi Sam,

Can you approve $18k for two contract iOS developers for
October-December?

- Why: our open iOS role has been unfilled for 10 weeks;
  without help the app slips from November to February.
- What: two contractors from Acme, $9k each, vetted
  by Priya.
- Deadline: Thursday, so they can start on 1 October.

Reply "yes" and I'll send the contracts for signature.

Thanks,
Alex
```

| Situation | Subject line pattern |
| :-------- | :------------------- |
| request | "[Action] by [date]?" — "Review pricing page by Fri?" |
| FYI | "FYI: [the news]" — "FYI: EU launch moved to 3 Nov" |
| decision made | "Decision: [what]" — "Decision: we're moving billing to Stripe" |
| urgent | "Urgent: [impact] — [ask]" — "Urgent: checkout down — need DB access" |
| intro | "Intro: [A] ↔ [B] ([why])" |

## Cold outreach

Cold emails compete with hundreds of others. The formula is **short, specific, easy yes**.

Michael Seibel (YC), "How to Email Early Stage Investors": sentence one, "Clearly explain what you do"; sentence
two, "Sell me on why I should be excited"; sentence three, "Ask for what you want". He adds: "Don't ask for a
phone call or a meeting. Let me escalate things." His listed mistakes include "Writing too much. Walls of text
result in slow/no replies."

| Principle | Detail |
| :-------- | :----- |
| relevance | one line proving this is for *them*, not a mail-merge (their post, product, problem) |
| specificity | numbers and names; "3,000 teams" beats "fast-growing" |
| small ask | a question, feedback, a 15-minute call at most; not "partnership" |
| easy yes | the reply can be one word |
| no flattery padding | one genuine specific compliment at most; skip "I'm a huge fan" |
| follow up once or twice | short, polite, adds something new; then stop |
| send from a real address | company domain; a real name |

| Before | After | Why |
| :----- | :---- | :-- |
| "I'm the founder of an innovative AI-powered platform revolutionising workflows." | "We built a tool that turns Zendesk tickets into Jira bugs automatically; 40 support teams use it." | specific product, real traction |
| "Would love to pick your brain sometime!" | "Would you tell me whether you'd pay \$20/seat for this? A one-word answer is plenty." | small, answerable ask |
| "Let me know if you'd be open to hopping on a call to explore synergies." | "If useful, I can send a 2-minute demo video. Want it?" | easy yes, no calendar cost |

## Pitches and one-liners

### Pitch structure

Sequoia's "Writing a Business Plan" outline, the standard skeleton for a pitch deck or narrative:

| # | Section | Sequoia's prompt |
| :- | :------ | :--------------- |
| 1 | Company purpose | "Define your company in a single declarative sentence." |
| 2 | Problem | "Describe the pain of your customer." |
| 3 | Solution | "Explain your eureka moment." |
| 4 | Why now? | "The best companies almost always have a clear why now?" |
| 5 | Market potential | "Identify your customer and your market." |
| 6 | Competition / alternatives | show "that you have a plan to win" |
| 7 | Business model | "How do you intend to thrive?" |
| 8 | Team | "Tell the story of your founders and key team members." |
| 9 | Financials | "If you have any, please include." |
| 10 | Vision | "If all goes well, what will you have built in five years?" |

Michael Seibel's "How to Pitch Your Company" reduces it to seven questions: what do you do; how big is the
market; what's your progress; what's your unique insight; what's your business model; who's on your team; what do
you want. His editing rule: "eliminate jargon, acronyms, marketing speak, and any ambiguous terms such as
'platform'", and "you don't need to sound cool. You need to be clear."

The compact narrative order for a written pitch: **problem → solution → why now → traction → market → team → ask**.
Lead with traction if it is your strongest card.

### The one-liner

Seibel's first answer is the model: start with the company name and what it does, e.g. "Socialcam is a mobile
app that makes it easy to take videos and share them with friends and family." He adds: "There's no need to set up
the problem, you can just get to the point."

```text
[Company] helps [specific who] [do what outcome]
by [how / mechanism].

Examples
Acme helps Shopify stores recover abandoned carts
by texting shoppers a one-tap checkout link.

Ledgerly helps two-person accounting firms close
the month in a day by reconciling bank feeds
automatically.
```

| Test | Pass |
| :--- | :--- |
| a smart outsider can repeat it | Seibel's "Email Test": email two sentences to a friend and ask them to explain it back |
| no buzzwords | no "platform", "AI-powered", "revolutionise", "ecosystem" |
| the customer is specific | "Shopify stores", not "businesses" |
| the outcome is concrete | "recover abandoned carts", not "drive growth" |

**"X for Y" one-liners** ("Twitch for cooking", an example from Seibel's own investor-email post) are fast when
both halves are well known and the analogy transfers the right thing. They fail when X is obscure, when X's
business model is the wrong one to imply, or when every competitor uses the same X. Follow the analogy with the
plain version.

## Copywriting formulas

Formulas are scaffolds for short persuasive copy: landing pages, ads, launch posts, cold emails.

| Formula | Steps | Best for | Example |
| :------ | :---- | :------- | :------ |
| **AIDA** | Attention → Interest → Desire → Action | ads, landing pages | "Still reconciling by hand? (A) Ledgerly matches 95% of bank lines automatically (I). Close the month in a day, not a week (D). Start free (A)." |
| **PAS** | Problem → Agitate → Solution | pain-driven products, cold email | "Deploys take 40 minutes. Every one blocks the team and invites a hotfix-on-hotfix. Our cache cuts builds to 4." |
| **BAB** | Before → After → Bridge | outcome-driven products, case studies | "Before: on-call pages at 3 a.m. After: the fix is a one-click rollback. Bridge: Canary runs every deploy against real traffic first." |
| **4 Us** | Useful, Urgent, Unique, Ultra-specific | headlines, subject lines | "Cut your AWS bill 30% before Q4 renewals (the 6 settings we changed)" |
| **FAB** | Feature → Advantage → Benefit | product pages, sales copy | "SSO (F) means no separate passwords (A), so offboarding takes one click and no one keeps access (B)." |

**AIDA's history** is murkier than its usual attribution. It is generally credited to advertising pioneer
E. St. Elmo Lewis around 1898, but an anonymous formula ("attract attention… interest and convince") appeared in
*Printers' Ink* in 1898, the four-step version was elaborated by others in the 1900s, and the acronym itself
appears in print around 1921. Reviews of hierarchy-of-effects models (e.g. Vakratsas and Ambler, 1999) find
little evidence that buyers move through the steps in order. Treat it as a checklist, not a model of the mind.

The origins of PAS, BAB, FAB and the 4 Us are copywriting folklore; none has a reliable single inventor.

## Headlines and titles

A title is a promise. It must be specific enough to select the right readers and honest enough that the body
delivers.

| Lever | Before | After |
| :---- | :----- | :---- |
| specificity | "Improving our infrastructure" | "How we cut deploy time from 40 minutes to 4" |
| numbers | "Some pricing lessons" | "7 pricing mistakes that cost us \$200k" |
| reader benefit | "Our new caching layer" | "Faster pages with no code changes" |
| the claim itself | "Thoughts on remote work" | "Remote teams need more writing, not more meetings" |
| audience selection | "A guide to Postgres" | "Postgres indexing for Rails developers" |
| question (sparingly) | "Microservices discussion" | "Do you actually need microservices?" |

| Curiosity | Clickbait |
| :-------- | :-------- |
| withholds the answer but promises a specific one | withholds the answer and the body doesn't have one |
| "Why our fastest-growing feature had no marketing" | "You won't believe what happened next" |
| the reader is glad they clicked | the reader feels tricked and remembers your name for it |

## Storytelling in persuasion

Stories carry pathos and make logos memorable. In business writing a story is usually one customer, one moment,
one change.

**The story spine**, created by improv teacher Kenn Adams and widely circulated through a list of Pixar
storytelling tips:

```text
Once upon a time...            (the world / the status quo)
Every day...                   (the routine)
But, one day...                (the event that breaks it)
Because of that...             (consequence)
Because of that...             (consequence)
Until, finally...              (the climax)
And, ever since then...        (the new normal)

Business version
Once upon a time, support teams copied tickets into Jira.
Every day, bugs were lost or duplicated.
One day, Maya's team missed a data-loss bug for a week.
Because of that, they lost their biggest customer.
Because of that, Maya built a script to sync tickets.
Until finally, every support team in the company used it.
And ever since, bugs reach engineering in minutes.
(Now it's a product. Here's what we're asking for...)
```

| Story device | Use |
| :----------- | :-- |
| **Before / After / Bridge** | show the reader's world now, the better world, then your product as the bridge |
| **Customer story** | one named (or anonymised but specific) customer with a real number |
| **Anecdote + data** | the story makes it vivid; the data proves it isn't a one-off |
| **Founder story** | only when it explains the unique insight; not biography |
| **Contrast** | "Most teams do X. We found Y." |

## Evidence and objections

### Evidence

| Type | Strength | Risk |
| :--- | :------- | :--- |
| data you measured | strong, specific | cherry-picking; show the baseline and the denominator |
| experiment / A/B test | strongest for causal claims | underpowered tests; state sample size |
| customer quote | vivid, credible | unrepresentative; say how many said similar |
| case study | concrete proof it works somewhere | survivorship; mention failures |
| expert or authority | borrows credibility | irrelevant authority; appeal-to-authority fallacy |
| social proof (logos, user counts) | reduces perceived risk | vanity metrics; readers check |
| analogy | makes the unfamiliar familiar | proves nothing; the analogy may break exactly where it matters |

Rules: **give the number, the baseline and the source**; round sensibly; prefer one strong piece of evidence over
five weak ones; never state a figure you cannot defend in the meeting.

Robert Cialdini's principles of influence (reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority,
liking, scarcity, and later unity) describe real shortcuts readers use. See
[cognitive biases](/thinking/mental-models/cognitive-biases/). Use them to present true things well, never to
fabricate urgency or consensus.

### Handling objections

Answer the strongest objection in the document before the reader raises it. Unanswered objections become the
meeting.

| Technique | How | Example |
| :-------- | :-- | :------ |
| **pre-empt** | raise it yourself, then answer it | "The obvious worry is lock-in. Our data export runs nightly to S3, so switching costs a week." |
| **concede and counter** | grant what is true, then show why it doesn't decide the question | "Yes, Stripe costs more per transaction. It still saves \$180k a year in audit and engineering." |
| **steelman** | state the other side at its strongest | "The best case for building in-house is control over checkout UX…" |
| **reframe the comparison** | compare to the real alternative, often the status quo | "The choice isn't Stripe vs perfect; it's Stripe vs a six-month delay." |
| **reduce the stakes** | make it a pilot or a reversible step | "Let's run it for one region for 60 days." |
| **FAQ section** | list and answer expected questions | the Amazon internal FAQ |

## Calls to action

| Rule | Before | After |
| :--- | :----- | :---- |
| one action | "Sign up, follow us, and read our blog" | "Start your free trial" |
| a verb plus the outcome | "Submit" | "Get my report" |
| specific commitment | "Let's chat sometime" | "Book a 15-minute call this week" |
| deadline with a reason | "Reply soon" | "Reply by Friday; the pricing changes on the 1st" |
| lower the cost | "Buy now" | "Try free for 14 days, no card" |
| say what happens next | "Contact sales" | "Get a quote within one business day" |

In internal documents the call to action is the **decision requested**: "I recommend option B and need your
approval by 30 September to hit the Q1 launch."

## Writing for skimmers

Jakob Nielsen's "How Users Read on the Web" (NN/g, 1997) found that "79 percent of our test users always scanned
any new page they came across; only 16 percent read word-by-word." Most business readers skim email and docs the
same way.

| Technique | Why |
| :-------- | :-- |
| BLUF in the first two lines | skimmers read the start and leave |
| descriptive headings that state claims | "Stripe saves \$180k a year" beats "Cost analysis" |
| bold the key phrase, not whole sentences | bold everything and nothing stands out |
| short paragraphs, one idea each | each paragraph is an entry point |
| tables for comparisons | options × criteria is the natural shape of a decision |
| numbered lists for sequences and counts | "three reasons" sets expectations |
| front-load sentences and list items | the first two words carry the scan |
| a summary box or TL;DR | the reader who reads nothing else gets the point |

## Persuasive essays and posts

Paul Graham's essays (see [Y Combinator](/startups/advice/y-combinator/)) are a model of informal persuasion:
plain words, a conversational voice, one idea pushed hard. In "How to Write Usefully" (2020) he argues an essay
should aim to be useful rather than merely persuasive: "Useful writing tells people something true and important
that they didn't already know, and tells them as unequivocally as possible." He adds: "Useful writing makes claims
that are as strong as they can be made without becoming false."

| Part | Job |
| :--- | :-- |
| Title | the claim or the question, specific |
| Hook (first paragraph) | the surprising claim, a concrete scene or the question the reader already has |
| Thesis | one sentence the whole piece defends |
| Body | 3–5 reasons or steps, each with evidence or an example; counterarguments addressed |
| Turn | the objection a thoughtful reader would raise, answered |
| Conclusion | what follows: the implication, the action, the new question; not a summary |

## Persuasion vs manipulation

The line: **persuasion helps the reader make a decision they would endorse with full information; manipulation
gets a decision they would reject if they saw how it was produced.**

| Persuasion | Manipulation |
| :--------- | :----------- |
| true evidence, selected fairly | cherry-picked or invented numbers |
| real deadlines with reasons | fake scarcity and countdown timers |
| states the downsides and risks | hides them in fine print |
| the reader's interest aligns with the ask | exploits a bias against the reader's interest |
| you would be comfortable if the reader saw your draft notes | you would not |

Besides ethics, manipulation is bad strategy for anyone who writes to the same readers twice: ethos is the
appeal that compounds.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
| :------ | :-- |
| writing about your content, not the reader's decision | start from "who decides what, by when" |
| burying the ask | first line, and repeat it at the end |
| several asks in one document | one ask; split the rest |
| background before the point | answer first, then SCQ |
| straw-man alternatives | include the strongest alternative and "do nothing" |
| ignoring the obvious objection | pre-empt it in the text or the FAQ |
| adjectives instead of evidence | "fast" → the number, the baseline, the source |
| buzzwords in pitches | Seibel's email test: a smart friend must be able to repeat it |
| bullet lists instead of reasoning | narrative prose for arguments; tables for data |
| clickbait titles | the title's promise must be kept by the body |
| manufactured urgency | a real deadline and its reason, or none |
| sending the first draft | write, wait, get a reviewer, rewrite |

## Templates

### Six-page narrative memo

```text
Title: [Recommendation as a sentence]
Author / date / decision needed by: [..]

1. Summary (1 paragraph)
   What we propose, why, what it costs, what we need.
2. Context
   Situation the reader agrees with. Key numbers.
3. Problem / opportunity
   The complication. Why it matters now (kairos).
4. Proposal
   What we will do; scope and non-goals; milestones.
5. Alternatives considered
   Each option, why it loses. Include "do nothing".
6. Risks and mitigations
   What could go wrong; what would change our mind.
7. Cost and resourcing
   People, money, time; reversibility.
8. Success metrics
   Metric, baseline, target, review date.
9. Ask
   The specific decision or resources requested.

Appendix: data, detailed financials, FAQ.
Rules: full sentences; no slides; <= 6 pages + appendix.
```

### PR/FAQ

```text
HEADING: [Product name] [does what] for [whom]
SUBHEADING: [Customer] can now [benefit] - [one sentence]

[CITY] - [Launch date] - [Company] today announced
[product], which [summary of benefit].

PROBLEM: Today, [customer] [struggles with X]. [Why it
hurts, in the customer's words.]

SOLUTION: [Product] [how it solves X]. Unlike [current
alternatives], it [key difference].

"[Why we built it]," said [spokesperson, title].

"[Specific benefit in plain words]," said [customer].

GETTING STARTED: [How to start in one or two sentences.]

EXTERNAL FAQ
Q: How much does it cost?
Q: How does it work?
Q: What if [common concern]?

INTERNAL FAQ
Q: How big is the market and how do we know?
Q: What does it cost to build and run?
Q: What are the biggest risks?
Q: What has to be true for this to succeed?
Q: Why us, why now?
```

### Decision doc

```text
DECISION: [Question] - Decider: [name] - By: [date]

CONTEXT (facts only)
[..]

OPTIONS
A. [..]   B. [..]   C. Do nothing

| Criterion | A | B | C |
| ...       |   |   |   |

RECOMMENDATION
[Option], because [top 2-3 reasons].

RISKS / WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND
[..]

REVERSIBILITY
[One-way or two-way door; cost to undo]
```

### Request email

```text
Subject: [Action] by [date]?

Hi [Name],

Can you [specific ask] by [date]?

- Why: [one line, with a number]
- What: [one line: scope, cost]
- If not: [consequence or alternative]

Reply "[yes]" and I'll [next step].

[Name]
```

### Cold email

```text
Subject: [Specific, relevant, short]

Hi [Name],

[One line showing why you're writing to *them*.]

[What you do, one sentence, no jargon.]
[Why it's interesting: best traction or insight.]

[Small, specific ask with an easy yes.]

[Name], [role], [company domain]

Follow-up (5-7 days later, same thread):
"Hi [Name], quick bump - [one new fact]. Worth a look?"
```

### Pitch narrative

```text
ONE-LINER  [Company] helps [who] [outcome] by [how].
PROBLEM    [Who] [pain], costing [number]. Today they
           [workaround], which fails because [..].
SOLUTION   [What it does]. [The insight others miss].
WHY NOW    [Shift: tech, regulation, behaviour, cost].
TRACTION   [Users/revenue/growth rate, time period].
MARKET     [Bottom-up: # customers x price].
COMPETITION [Alternatives and why we win].
MODEL      [Who pays, how much, how often].
TEAM       [Founders; the one relevant credential].
ASK        [Amount, what it buys, milestones].
```

### Landing page (AIDA)

```text
HEADLINE (Attention)   [Outcome] for [who] [in time/cost]
SUBHEAD (Interest)     [How it works, one sentence]
PROOF (Desire)         [Number] [customer logos] [quote]
BENEFITS (Desire)      3 x [feature -> benefit]
OBJECTION              [Top worry] - [answer]
CTA (Action)           [Verb + outcome] [risk reducer]
```
