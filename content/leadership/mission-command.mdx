Leading by intent instead of by instruction: the Prussian origins (*Auftragstaktik*), the US Army's seven principles,
how to write commander's intent, the five-paragraph order, troop leading procedures, backbriefs and rehearsals,
after-action reviews, and how to run a startup this way without losing control of the things that must stay
centralised. The decision cycle this speeds up is the [OODA loop](/leadership/ooda-loop/); the ownership mindset it
needs is [Extreme Ownership](/leadership/extreme-ownership/); delegation and one-on-ones in everyday management are
in [management](/leadership/management/).

## The idea in one paragraph

Plans break on contact with reality, and the people closest to the problem see the change first. **Mission
command** gives them the *purpose* and the *desired result*, the resources and the limits, and leaves the *how* to
them, so that when the plan stops fitting they can act without waiting for permission. It only works with competent
people, mutual trust and shared understanding, which is why it is as much a culture as a technique.

| | Detailed command | Mission command |
| :-- | :--------------- | :-------------- |
| Assumes | the world is predictable enough to plan in detail | the world is uncertain; plans will break |
| Orders say | what to do, step by step | what to achieve and why; minimum necessary constraints |
| Decisions made | at the top | at the lowest level with the information |
| Information flows | up for approval, down as instructions | intent down, situation up, sideways between peers |
| When the plan fails | stop, report, wait for new orders | act within intent, report when able |
| Strength | synchronisation, predictability, control | speed, adaptability, initiative |
| Weakness | slow, brittle, overloads the top | needs trust and training; can desynchronise |
| Business analogue | approval chains, detailed specs, micromanagement | goals with guard-rails, empowered teams |

## Origins: Auftragstaktik

| When | What happened |
| :--- | :------------ |
| 1806 | Napoleon destroys the Prussian army at Jena–Auerstedt |
| 1807–1810s | reformers (Scharnhorst, Gneisenau, later Clausewitz) build a professional officer corps and a General Staff that educates officers to think, not just obey |
| 1857–1888 | **Helmuth von Moltke the Elder** is Chief of the Prussian (later German) General Staff; wins the wars of 1864, 1866 and 1870–71 with short directives and wide latitude for subordinates |
| 1871 | Moltke's essay *Über Strategie* (On Strategy): "No plan of operations extends with any certainty beyond the first contact with the main hostile force" |
| 1890s–1914 | German writers debate "free method" (*freies Verfahren*) and "mission method" (*Auftragsverfahren*); the word *Auftragstaktik* emerges from this debate |
| 1987 | British Army announces it will adopt mission command; an internal review in 2004 found its orders had become *more* detailed instead |
| 2003 | US Army FM 6-0 codifies the term "mission command" |
| 2012 → 2019 | ADP 6-0 renames the whole command function "mission command" (2012), then restores "command and control" and defines mission command as the Army's *approach* to it (2019) |
| July 2026 | current ADP 6-0 *Command and Control* supersedes the 2019 edition; mission command and its seven principles are unchanged; adds dispersed command posts, data and multidomain operations |
| today | the Bundeswehr calls it *Führen mit Auftrag* (leading by mission) |

The Moltke line in German reads "Kein Operationsplan reicht mit einiger Sicherheit über das erste Zusammentreffen
mit der feindlichen Hauptmacht hinaus" (Hughes and Bell translation, *Moltke on the Art of War*, 1993). "No plan
survives contact with the enemy" is a later **paraphrase**, not his wording. His point is narrower and more useful:
plan in detail up to the first decisive clash, and beyond that plan only the intent and the options.

ADP 6-0's favourite American example is **Grant's letter to Sherman of 4 April 1864**: "I do not propose to lay down
for you a plan of campaign, but simply to lay down the work it is desirable to have done and leave you free to
execute it in your own way. Submit to me, however, as early as you can, your plan of operation." That is intent,
freedom of action and a backbrief in three sentences. Sherman's reply of 10 April restated the aim (knock
Johnston's army out and damage the enemy's war resources, keeping Johnston too busy to reinforce elsewhere) and
promised not to "let side issues draw me off from your main plan".

## The seven principles (ADP 6-0)

ADP 6-0 defines **mission command** as "the Army's approach to command and control that empowers subordinate
decision making and decentralized execution appropriate to the situation". Its principles, identical in the 2019
and 2026 editions:

| Principle | What doctrine means | Business translation | Warning sign it is missing |
| :-------- | :------------------ | :------------------- | :------------------------- |
| **Competence** | tactically and technically competent leaders and teams, built by training and education | people are trained and experienced enough to be trusted with the decision | "empowering" someone who has never done it and has no support |
| **Mutual trust** | trust up and down, earned through shared experience, two-way communication and keeping promises | the boss backs good-faith decisions; the team tells the boss the truth | people ask permission for things inside their authority |
| **Shared understanding** | a common picture of the situation, the problem and the approach, pushed to the lowest level | everyone sees the same metrics, customer feedback and strategy | teams surprised by decisions others consider obvious |
| **Commander's intent** | a clear, concise statement of purpose, key tasks and end state | the why and what-good-looks-like for a project or quarter | work that meets the spec and misses the point |
| **Mission orders** | directives that state the results to attain, not how to achieve them | goals and constraints, not task lists | 30-step plans; "just do what the ticket says" |
| **Disciplined initiative** | the duty to act within intent when orders no longer fit, the situation changes or an opportunity appears | people fix the problem and tell you, rather than wait | people follow a failing plan because it is the plan |
| **Risk acceptance** | commanders deliberately accept and underwrite risk, including subordinates' good-faith mistakes | leaders absorb the cost of reasonable bets that fail | the first honest mistake gets someone publicly punished |

### Disciplined initiative: when to depart from the plan

ADP 6-0 tells subordinates to follow the plan until it no longer fits, then act, and to weigh two things:

1. Do the benefits of the action outweigh the risk of **desynchronising** the overall operation?
2. Will the action **further the higher commander's intent**?

If there is time, tell the boss the new situation and the recommended action first. If there is not, act within
intent and report when able. Doctrine's tie-breaker: if in doubt whether to wait or seize a fleeting opportunity,
act if you can do so within the intent.

## Commander's intent

ADP 6-0 (quoting JP 3-0): "a clear and concise expression of the purpose of an operation and the desired objectives
and military end state". It includes three parts, and must be understood by people **two levels down**.

| Part | Meaning | Test |
| :--- | :------ | :--- |
| **Purpose** | the broader why, in relation to the higher leader's intent (not a restatement of the mission's "why") | if the plan collapses, does this tell people which way to go? |
| **Key tasks** | the few things the whole force must do to reach the end state; not a task list for any one team | would dropping one of these make success unlikely? |
| **End state** | the conditions that exist when the operation is complete: ours, the competitor's, the customer's, the market's | could two people independently agree whether we got there? |

Doctrine's style rules: the leader writes it personally, delivers it face to face where possible, keeps it short
enough to remember, and includes the purpose of each key task so people can improvise sensibly.

### Business intent template

```text
INTENT: <project / quarter / initiative>

PURPOSE  Why this matters to the company's goal this year,
         in one or two sentences. Link to the level above:
         "So that <company goal>, we need to..."

KEY TASKS (3 max, each with its purpose)
  1. <task> - so that <reason>
  2. <task> - so that <reason>
  3. <task> - so that <reason>

END STATE (observable conditions when we are done)
  - Customers: ...
  - Product/system: ...
  - Team/company: ...

CONSTRAINTS  Must do / must not do (budget, legal, brand,
             security, dates that cannot move).
DECISIONS    You decide: ...   Check with me: ...
BACKBRIEF    Tell me your plan by <date>.
```

### Good and bad intent

| Weak intent | Why it fails | Stronger intent |
| :---------- | :----------- | :-------------- |
| "Launch v2 by June." | a date and an output; no purpose or success condition | "Launch v2 so that mid-market customers can self-serve onboarding. Done when 70% of new mid-market accounts activate without a CSM call, with no increase in support tickets per account. Date matters less than quality: we'd rather slip two weeks than launch something that needs hand-holding." |
| "Grow revenue." | unbounded; every action qualifies | "Grow net new ARR 25% this quarter without discounting over 15%, so we reach the Series B milestone with healthy unit economics. Key tasks: fix the trial-to-paid drop at step 3; land 5 reference customers in healthcare." |
| "Improve reliability." | nobody can tell when it is done | "Get checkout availability to 99.95% for a full month so enterprise prospects stop raising reliability in procurement. We'll trade feature work for it, not headcount." |

## Mission orders and levels of control

A **mission order** is not a separate kind of order but a way of writing one: state the mission, task organisation,
intent, concept of operations, tasks to subordinate units and essential coordinating instructions, with the
emphasis on purpose. It never repeats what standard procedures already cover, and never tells a subordinate *how*
to do their task. The right level of detail depends on the situation.

**When tighter, centralised control is correct.** ADP 6-0 lists operations needing close synchronisation (combined
arms breaches, air assaults, river crossings) as ones to control more tightly, and pursuits or movements to contact
as ones to loosen. Factors that push towards more control: an enemy's capability, the degree of synchronisation
needed, constraints from above, risk, legal and ethical ambiguity, rules of engagement, and low cohesion, training,
trust or shared understanding.

| Situation in a company | Control | Why |
| :--------------------- | :------ | :-- |
| security breach, legal or regulatory exposure, press crisis | tight, one incident commander | errors are irreversible; one voice |
| database cutover, pricing change, coordinated launch | tight during the synchronised phase, loose before and after | many teams must move at the same moment |
| new team, new hires, or someone doing this for the first time | tighter, with coaching | competence and trust not yet built |
| a 3-person company | little formal structure needed | shared understanding is automatic |
| product discovery, sales prospecting, content, most engineering work | loose: intent, constraints and a check-in | local knowledge beats central planning |
| an experiment with a small, capped downside | loosest | the point is to learn fast |

Mission orders do not mean no supervision. Commanders remain accountable, check on subordinates and intervene when
needed; in training, they deliberately intervene only when necessary so people learn.

## The five-paragraph order

The Army standard is the five-paragraph **operation order (OPORD)**: Situation, Mission, Execution, Sustainment,
Command and signal. The Marine Corps version is taught as **SMEAC**, with the fourth paragraph "Administration and
logistics".

| Para | Military content | Business version |
| :--- | :--------------- | :--------------- |
| 1. Situation | area of operations, enemy forces, friendly forces (higher unit's mission and intent, adjacent units), civil considerations, attachments, assumptions | market and customers; competitors; our position; what other teams are doing that affects us; assumptions |
| 2. Mission | who, what, when, where, why, in one sentence | "The growth team (who) increases trial-to-paid conversion from 8% to 11% (what) across all self-serve plans (where) by 30 September (when) so that we hit the ARR target without more sales hires (why)." |
| 3. Execution | commander's intent; concept of operations; tasks to subordinate units; coordinating instructions | intent (purpose, key tasks, end state); the plan in phases; who owns what; shared rules (dates, review points, decision thresholds) |
| 4. Sustainment / admin and logistics | supply, maintenance, medical, personnel | budget, tools, vendors, hiring, on-call, who covers absences |
| 5. Command and signal | where the commander is, succession of command, communications plan | owner and deputy; decision rights; meeting cadence; channels; escalation path; how progress is reported |

### Business OPORD template

```text
1. SITUATION
   Customers/market: ...
   Competitors ("enemy"): ...
   Us ("friendly"): higher-level goal and intent: ...
     adjacent teams and what they're doing: ...
   Assumptions: ...
2. MISSION
   <Who> <does what> <where> <by when> <so that why>.
3. EXECUTION
   Intent: purpose / key tasks / end state
   Concept: phase 1 ..., phase 2 ..., phase 3 ...
   Tasks: Team A ... so that ...; Team B ... so that ...
   Coordinating instructions: review dates, thresholds
   ("if conversion < 9% by week 6, we regroup"), rules.
4. SUSTAINMENT
   Budget, tools, people, vendors, on-call.
5. COMMAND AND SIGNAL
   Owner: ___  Deputy: ___  Decides alone: ___
   Escalate: ___  Cadence: weekly 30 min, Tue
   Channel: #proj-___   Status: Friday written update
```

A warning order (**WARNORD**) is the short heads-up issued early so people can start preparing before the full
order; a fragmentary order (**FRAGORD**) changes an existing order without reissuing it. In a company: the Slack
message on Monday ("we're going to do X, full plan Thursday, start thinking about Y") and the mid-project change note.

## Troop leading procedures

FM 5-0 (2024) sets out eight **troop leading procedures (TLP)** for small units without a staff. Steps 1–3 are
usually in order, 3–7 are sequenced as needed, and step 8 runs throughout.

| Step | Military | Condensed business planning |
| :--- | :------- | :-------------------------- |
| 1 | Receive the mission | get the goal; assess the time available |
| 2 | Issue a warning order | tell the team what's coming so they can start preparing |
| 3 | Make a tentative plan | analyse the problem; sketch a plan |
| 4 | Initiate movement | start the long-lead work now (hiring, vendor contracts, data access) |
| 5 | Conduct reconnaissance | go and look: talk to customers, read the code, check the data |
| 6 | Complete the plan | refine with what you learned; write it up |
| 7 | Issue the order | brief it face to face; take questions; confirmation briefs |
| 8 | Supervise and refine | backbriefs, rehearsals, check-ins, adjust |

**The one-third, two-thirds rule** (FM 5-0): use at most one-third of the time before execution for your own
planning and leave two-thirds for your subordinates to plan and prepare theirs. In a company: if the quarter starts
in 3 weeks, the leadership plan and intent should be out within a week, not on the last Friday.

## Backbriefs, confirmation briefs and rehearsals

| Tool | What it is | Business version |
| :--- | :--------- | :--------------- |
| **Confirmation brief** | immediately after receiving an order, the subordinate briefs back their understanding of the intent, their tasks and purposes, and how they relate to others | "Tell me in your own words what we're trying to achieve and what your team owns." (2 minutes, end of the kickoff) |
| **Backbrief** | later, the subordinate briefs how they intend to accomplish the mission; the leader checks it fits the intent and the plans of adjacent teams | each team lead presents their plan to the leader and the other leads a week after kickoff |
| **Rehearsal** | walking through the plan before execution; from a key-leader talk-through on a map to a full-dress run | a launch dry-run, a game day, an incident drill, a mock customer demo |

### Bungay's three gaps

Stephen Bungay, *The Art of Action* (2011), reframes mission command for business around three gaps between plans,
actions and outcomes, and argues that the instinctive fix for each makes it worse:

| Gap | Between | Instinctive (wrong) response | Mission command response |
| :-- | :------ | :--------------------------- | :----------------------- |
| **Knowledge gap** | what we'd like to know and what we actually know | gather more information, plan in more detail | don't plan beyond what circumstances allow; define and communicate the intent |
| **Alignment gap** | what we want people to do and what they actually do | give more detailed instructions | let each level work out how to achieve the intent and **backbrief** it |
| **Effects gap** | what we expect our actions to achieve and what they actually achieve | add more controls and metrics | give people freedom to adjust their actions in line with the intent |

He calls the resulting approach **directed opportunism**: strategy sets the direction; people seize opportunities
within it.

### Backbrief agenda (45 minutes, one team)

```text
1. Restate the higher intent in your words.       (5)
2. Your mission and intent for your team.          (5)
3. Your plan: phases, owners, key dates.          (10)
4. Dependencies on other teams; what you need.     (5)
5. Assumptions and the biggest risks.              (5)
6. Decision points: what triggers a change.        (5)
7. Leader and peers: questions, conflicts, gaps.  (10)
Output: agreed changes to the plan or the intent.
```

## After-action reviews

The US Army's current guide is TC 7-0.1 *After Action Reviews* (February 2025); the older TC 25-20 (1993) popularised
the familiar four-question form. An AAR is "a guided analysis of an organization's performance, conducted at
appropriate times during and at the conclusion of a training event or operation, with the objective of improving
future performance". Two rules from TC 7-0.1: an AAR **is not a critique**, and it **does not grade success or
failure**.

| Question | TC 7-0.1 section | What happens |
| :------- | :--------------- | :----------- |
| **What was supposed to happen?** | commander's mission and intent | the leader (or subordinates) restate the plan and intent |
| **What actually happened?** | summary of key events | build one agreed timeline from many perspectives and objective data before any analysis |
| **Why did it happen?** | root causes | open-ended questions; causes emerge from the discussion, not from the facilitator |
| **What do we sustain and improve?** | sustaining / improving performance | specific actions, owners and dates; update procedures |

### Facilitation guide

<Steps>
<Step title="Before">

- Hold it during or immediately after the event; the shorter the gap, the better the recall.
- Pick 3–5 focus topics in advance; gather objective data (timeline, dashboards, logs, recordings).
- Choose a facilitator who was not the decision-maker, if possible.

</Step>
<Step title="Open (5 minutes)">

State the rules: participation expected; not a critique; no grading; rank does not win arguments; we're here to
improve, not to assign blame. The senior person goes first with something they would do differently.

</Step>
<Step title="Walk the four questions (30–45 minutes)">

Establish what was supposed to happen, then build the agreed timeline, then ask why. Ask open questions of the most
junior people first: "What did you see when the alert fired?" beats "Why didn't you roll back?". Keep a visible list
of causes and of things that went well.

</Step>
<Step title="Close (10 minutes)">

Agree actions with an owner and a date each; record them; decide what changes in the standard procedure or
template. Summarise in writing within 24 hours.

</Step>
<Step title="Follow up">

Track actions to completion in the normal work system; review the list at the next AAR. An AAR whose actions never
get done teaches people that AARs are theatre.

</Step>
</Steps>

### AAR record

```text
EVENT: ______________  DATE: ______  FACILITATOR: ______
1. Intended: mission, intent, success criteria
2. Actual: timeline (time - event - source)
3. Why: causes (systems, information, decisions, luck)
   Went well and why:
4. Sustain: ...
   Improve: action - owner - due date
5. Changes to procedures/templates: ...
```

### Blameless postmortems

Google's SRE book (chapter 15, "Postmortem Culture: Learning from Failure") is the software form of the AAR.
Postmortems are triggered by criteria agreed in advance (user-visible downtime beyond a threshold, any data loss,
on-call intervention such as a rollback, resolution time beyond a threshold, a monitoring failure), and they must be
**blameless**: focused on contributing causes, assuming everyone acted with good intentions on the information they
had. If people are punished for what a postmortem reveals, they stop revealing things.

## Pre-mortems and red teaming

| Tool | What it is | Use before |
| :--- | :--------- | :--------- |
| **Pre-mortem** | Gary Klein (*Harvard Business Review*, 2007): imagine the project has already failed and ask what went wrong | committing to any big plan; step 6 of TLP |
| **Red team** | an independent group that challenges plans, assumptions and the leader's picture, often by thinking as the adversary | strategy decisions, security, major bets |

The US Army formalised red teaming after a 2003 Defense Science Board report: it stood up a service-level red team
in 2004, and the University of Foreign Military and Cultural Studies (UFMCS, the "Red Team University") at Fort
Leavenworth trains red-team leaders and members. For a startup, the cheap version is a named devil's advocate for
each major decision and an outsider (advisor, friendly customer) asked to attack the plan. How to run both:
[decision-making](/thinking/mental-models/decision-making/#pre-mortem).

## Two levels up, one level down

The doctrine behind the slogan:

- **Intent nests.** Each leader's intent nests within the intent **one and two echelons above** (FM 5-0), and
  understanding intent two echelons up is what lets people act sensibly when their own boss is unreachable
  (ADP 6-0).
- **Intent carries down.** A commander's intent must be easy to remember and clearly understood **two echelons
  below** (ADP 6-0).

The common rule of thumb that follows: **know your boss's boss's goal, and know your team's work in detail.**

| Level | You should know | Startup example for an engineering lead |
| :---- | :-------------- | :-------------------------------------- |
| two up | the company's goal and why | "reach \$5M ARR with healthy margins before the next raise" |
| one up | your boss's intent and constraints | VP Eng: "cut infrastructure cost 30% without hurting reliability" |
| you | your mission and intent | "move batch jobs to spot instances by Q3" |
| one down | each team member's tasks, blockers and skills | who owns the scheduler rewrite, who needs help with Terraform |
| two down | enough to check the intent survived translation | ask a junior engineer why the migration matters |

### Span of control

ADP 6-0 defines **span of control** as the number of subordinates or activities under one commander, and gives no
magic number: in fluid, fast-changing situations a commander can effectively supervise fewer subordinates; more
subordinates add flexibility. Narrowing the span adds layers, and more layers slow information up and down; widening
it flattens the organisation but overloads the leader. *Extreme Ownership* puts the practical limit at around six to
ten people. For startups, the usual failure is a founder with 15 direct reports who has become the bottleneck for
every decision: add a layer or push decisions down with explicit intent.

## Applying it to a startup

### Writing intent for a quarter

```text
Q3 INTENT (CEO, 20-person B2B SaaS)

PURPOSE   Prove we can sell to mid-market without founder-led
          sales, so the Series A story is "repeatable growth",
          not "great founders".
KEY TASKS
  1. Close 12 mid-market deals with no founder on the call
     - so that we know the sales motion is transferable.
  2. Cut onboarding to under 7 days - so that deals don't
     stall between signature and value.
  3. Keep gross churn under 2%/month - so that new growth
     isn't hiding a leak.
END STATE Two AEs each closing 1+ deal a month without
          founders; onboarding median under 7 days; a
          documented playbook a new AE can follow.
CONSTRAINTS No discounts over 20% without CEO sign-off.
          Security review answers must come from eng.
DECISIONS Team leads decide their own plans, hires within
          budget, tooling under $500/month.
BACKBRIEF Each lead presents their plan by 10 July.
```

### Delegation levels

Jurgen Appelo's seven delegation levels (from *Management 3.0*, 2011) make "decide within intent" explicit. Agree
the level per decision type, not per person.

| Level | Name | Meaning |
| :---- | :--- | :------ |
| 1 | Tell | I decide and tell you |
| 2 | Sell | I decide and try to convince you |
| 3 | Consult | I ask for your input, then decide |
| 4 | Agree | we decide together |
| 5 | Advise | I give advice, you decide |
| 6 | Inquire | you decide, then tell me |
| 7 | Delegate | you decide; I don't need to know |

A typical early-stage map: pricing and hiring bar at 3–4; feature scope within the roadmap at 6; bug fixes, tooling
and internal process at 7. More in [management](/leadership/management/#delegation).

### A mission-command operating rhythm

| Cadence | Mechanism | Mission command purpose |
| :------ | :-------- | :---------------------- |
| quarterly | written intent from the CEO; each lead writes nested intent; backbriefs | intent and alignment |
| weekly | 30-minute leads sync: what changed, what I decided, what I need | shared understanding; disciplined initiative reported up |
| daily | metrics everyone can see | shared understanding at the lowest level |
| after every launch, incident or lost deal | AAR or blameless postmortem | learning; trust |
| as needed | FRAGORD-style change note: what changed, new intent if any | keep the intent current without re-planning everything |

## Failure modes

| Failure | What it looks like | Fix |
| :------ | :----------------- | :-- |
| **Intent too vague** | "make the product great"; people optimise for different things | purpose, 3 key tasks, an observable end state; test by asking two people if they'd agree it's done |
| **Intent that is really a task list** | ten bullet points of what to do | cut to the why and what-done-looks-like; move tasks into the teams' own plans |
| **No shared context** | intent is clear but people lack the information to apply it | open dashboards, customer calls, strategy docs; push information down, not just intent |
| **Micromanagement dressed as intent** | "you own it" followed by daily detailed direction and overrides | agree the decision level up front; intervene only on the pre-agreed triggers |
| **Abdication dressed as empowerment** | intent issued, then no backbrief, no check-in, no support | backbriefs, rehearsals and supervision are part of mission command, not a violation of it |
| **Initiative punished** | the first good-faith mistake is criticised in public | underwrite honest mistakes; review them in a blameless AAR |
| **Competence assumed** | juniors given senior-level latitude with no training | tighten control for new people and new problems; loosen as competence is proven |
| **Desynchronisation** | teams each act sensibly and collide (two launches the same day, conflicting promises to a customer) | coordinating instructions; peers backbrief together; tighter control during synchronised phases |
| **Plans that are too detailed to adapt** | the plan is followed after it stopped making sense | plan in detail only to the first contact; beyond that, intent and options |
| **AARs as theatre** | reviews held, actions never done | owners and dates; track in the normal work system; revisit next time |
