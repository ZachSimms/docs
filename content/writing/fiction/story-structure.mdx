Cat!, the Hero's Journey, the Story Circle, seven-point structure, kishōtenketsu), the MICE quotient, try/fail cycles,

[character](/writing/fiction/character/); the scene and sentence level is in [scenes and prose](/writing/fiction/scene-prose/).
Setting and magic systems that the plot runs on are in [worldbuilding fundamentals](/writing/worldbuilding/fundamentals/) and
[magic and technology](/writing/worldbuilding/magic-technology/); branching and player-driven stories are in

## What structure is for

Structure is the order in which a reader learns things and the causal chain that links events. Every model below is a
description of what has worked, not a law. Use them to diagnose a draft, not to fill in blanks.

| Job | What structure does | Symptom when it fails |
| :-- | :------------------ | :-------------------- |
| Causality | each event is caused by the last and causes the next | episodic "and then" plot; reader can skip chapters without losing anything |
| Expectation | sets up a question the reader wants answered | reader does not know what the story is about by the end of the first act |
| Escalation | stakes and difficulty rise | the middle feels flat; the climax is easier than earlier obstacles |
| Change | something (situation, character, knowledge) is different at the end | ending feels arbitrary or "nothing happened" |
| Proportion | the right amount of space for each part | 40% setup; rushed climax; epilogue longer than the finale |
| Satisfaction | payoffs match promises | twist feels like a cheat; set-up threads dropped |

**Causality test ("therefore/but").** Trey Parker and Matt Stone, guest-teaching Ken Liotti's storytelling class at
NYU's Tisch School for mtvU's *Stand In* (2011), said that if the words "and then" belong between the beats of an
outline, the story is in trouble; the words between beats should be "therefore" or "but". Write the outline as one
sentence per beat and test each join.

| Join | Meaning | Example |
| :--- | :------ | :------ |
| and then | sequence only | she arrives in the city **and then** she visits the market **and then** she meets a thief |
| therefore | consequence | she arrives penniless, **therefore** she sells her mother's ring at the market |
| but | complication | she sells the ring, **but** the buyer recognises the family crest and follows her |

Aristotle made the same point about 2,300 years earlier: in the *Poetics* (ch. 9) he calls plots whose episodes follow
one another without probable or necessary sequence episodic ("epeisodic" in Butcher's translation) and rates them the
worst kind.

## Aristotle's Poetics

The *Poetics* (c. 335 BCE) is lecture notes on tragedy and epic. Its plot ideas still underpin Western story theory.

| Concept | Greek | Meaning | Use today |
| :------ | :---- | :------ | :-------- |
| Plot | *mythos* | the arrangement of incidents; "the soul of a tragedy" (Butcher trans.), ranked above character | plot is what characters *do*, not a list of events |
| Whole | — | beginning (needs nothing before it), middle (follows and is followed), end (follows, needs nothing after) | the opening must not need a prologue to make sense; the ending must not leave the central question open |
| Unity of action | — | one complete action, not everything that happened to one person | cut events that do not bear on the central question |
| Magnitude | — | long enough to allow a change of fortune, short enough to be held in memory as a whole | length should serve the change, not the page count |
| Probability | *eikos* | events follow by probability or necessity | coincidence may create problems, never solve them |
| Complication and unravelling | *desis*, *lusis* | everything up to the turning point, then everything after it | the root of "rising and falling action" |
| Reversal | *peripeteia* | action produces the opposite of what was intended | the plan that makes things worse |
| Recognition | *anagnorisis* | a change from ignorance to knowledge | the reveal; best when it coincides with the reversal (Oedipus) |
| Suffering | *pathos* | a destructive or painful act | the cost of the climax |
| Simple vs complex plot | — | complex plots contain reversal, recognition or both; Aristotle prefers them | a climax that turns on a discovery lands harder |

Aristotle also says the unravelling should arise from the plot itself rather than from a god lowered on stage
(*deus ex machina*, *Poetics* ch. 15). The advice survives unchanged: see [structural problems](#structural-problems-and-fixes).

## Freytag's pyramid

Gustav Freytag's *Die Technik des Dramas* (1863) analysed five-act tragedy (Sophocles, Shakespeare, German classical
drama) into five parts and three "moments". It describes tragedy well and modern novels poorly: most modern stories put
the climax near the end, not in the middle.

<Diagram src="/images/diagrams/fiction-freytag.svg" label="Freytag's pyramid: a flat introduction line, then a straight rising line from the exciting force up to the climax at the apex in the middle, then a straight falling line (the return or fall) down to the catastrophe on the right. The tragic force is marked just after the apex and the force of final suspense just before the end of the fall." caption="Freytag's pyramid: five parts and three moments of five-act tragedy" />

| Part | German | What happens | Modern equivalent |
| :--- | :----- | :----------- | :---------------- |
| Introduction | *Einleitung* | place, time, situation, characters | exposition, setup |
| Exciting force (moment) | *erregendes Moment* | the event that starts the struggle | inciting incident |
| Rise | *Steigerung* | complications build | rising action |
| Climax | *Höhepunkt* | the hero's fortunes peak and turn | midpoint reversal (in modern terms) |
| Tragic force (moment) | *tragisches Moment* | the event that starts the fall | the midpoint's consequences |
| Return or fall | *Fall / Umkehr* | opposing forces gain | "bad guys close in" |
| Force of final suspense | *Moment der letzten Spannung* | a last hope of escape | false hope before the end |
| Catastrophe | *Katastrophe* | the hero's downfall, usually death | climax and resolution |

Freytag considered the exciting force necessary and the other two moments optional.

## Three-act structure

Three acts is the least controversial model: a beginning that poses a dramatic question, a middle that complicates it,
an end that answers it. Syd Field turned it into a screenwriting paradigm in *Screenplay: The Foundations of
Screenwriting* (1979): **setup**, **confrontation**, **resolution**, joined by two **plot points**.

| Part | Field's label | Conventional share | In a 120-page script | Contents |
| :--- | :------------ | :----------------- | :------------------- | :------- |
| Act I | Setup | ~25% | pp. 1–30 | world, protagonist, want, inciting incident, dramatic question |
| Plot point I | — | ~20–25% | around pp. 20–30 | an event that spins the story in a new direction and locks the protagonist in |
| Act II | Confrontation | ~50% | pp. 30–90 | obstacles, escalation, subplots; usually a midpoint reversal |
| Plot point II | — | ~70–75% | around pp. 80–90 | event that launches the endgame, often a low point or new information |
| Act III | Resolution | ~25% | pp. 90–120 | climax, answer to the dramatic question, new equilibrium |

These page numbers are **conventions** from 1970s–80s screenwriting (one page ≈ one minute of screen time), not rules.
Field's later books add a **midpoint** around page 60 and "pinch" scenes that remind the audience of the antagonist
either side of it. Novelists use the percentages, not the pages.

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :------ |
| Inciting incident | the event that disturbs the status quo and creates the problem; often 10–15% in |
| Dramatic question | the yes/no question the climax answers ("Will she get the ring back?") |
| Plot point / turning point | a change that sends the story in a new direction |
| Midpoint | a reversal or revelation that raises stakes or flips the protagonist from reactive to proactive |
| Climax | the decisive confrontation; the protagonist's choice answers the dramatic question |
| Denouement | the unravelling after the climax: consequences, new normal |

Criticism: "three acts" describes almost anything (beginning, middle, end), so it gives little help with the long second
act, which is where most drafts fail. The finer-grained models below exist to fill that gap.

## Save the Cat! beat sheet

Blake Snyder's *Save the Cat!* (2005) split three acts into 15 beats with page numbers for a 110-page screenplay. Jessica
Brody's *Save the Cat! Writes a Novel* (2018) converted them to percentages for novels. The book is named after the
moment early on where the hero does something likeable (see [character](/writing/fiction/character/)).

| # | Beat | Script page (Snyder) | Novel position (Brody) | What happens |
| :- | :--- | :------------------- | :--------------------- | :----------- |
| 1 | Opening Image | 1 | 0–1% | a snapshot of the "before" world and hero |
| 2 | Theme Stated | 5 | 5% | someone (usually not the hero) states the lesson the hero needs |
| 3 | Set-Up | 1–10 | 1–10% | hero's life, flaws, what is missing; the people who will matter |
| 4 | Catalyst | 12 | 10% | the life-changing event (inciting incident) |
| 5 | Debate | 12–25 | 10–20% | the hero hesitates: should I go? can I? |
| 6 | Break into Two | 25 | 20% | the hero chooses to enter the new world or plan |
| 7 | B Story | 30 | 22% | a new relationship (love, mentor, friend) that carries the theme |
| 8 | Fun and Games | 30–55 | 20–50% | "the promise of the premise": the scenes the trailer is made of |
| 9 | Midpoint | 55 | 50% | a false victory or false defeat; stakes rise; clocks appear |
| 10 | Bad Guys Close In | 55–75 | 50–75% | external enemies regroup, internal flaws undermine the team |
| 11 | All Is Lost | 75 | 75% | the lowest point; often a "whiff of death" (a mentor dies, a plan fails) |
| 12 | Dark Night of the Soul | 75–85 | 75–80% | the hero wallows and then finds the lesson |
| 13 | Break into Three | 85 | 80% | the A and B stories combine into the solution |
| 14 | Finale | 85–110 | 80–99% | the hero applies the lesson; the old world is replaced |
| 15 | Final Image | 110 | 99–100% | the opposite of the opening image, proving change happened |

Strengths: it names the middle (Fun and Games, Midpoint, Bad Guys Close In) and makes the theme structural. Weaknesses:
it is tuned to Hollywood genre films, and followed literally it produces formula. Hitting the percentages exactly is
neither necessary nor what readers notice; hitting the *functions* in roughly the right order is.

## The Hero's Journey

Joseph Campbell's *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949) argued that many myths share one pattern, the **monomyth**:
departure, initiation, return. George Lucas has said that reading Campbell showed him his *Star Wars* drafts were
following classical motifs. Christopher Vogler, a Disney story consultant, condensed Campbell into a 1985 studio memo
("A Practical Guide to *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*") and then *The Writer's Journey* (1992; later editions 1998,
2007, 2020), with 12 stages built for screenwriters.

### Campbell's 17 stages

| Part | # | Stage | Short meaning |
| :--- | :- | :---- | :------------ |
| Departure | 1 | The Call to Adventure | a herald or event summons the hero to the unknown |
| | 2 | Refusal of the Call | the hero declines out of fear, duty or comfort |
| | 3 | Supernatural Aid | a protective figure gives an amulet, advice or help |
| | 4 | The Crossing of the First Threshold | the hero passes a guardian into the unknown |
| | 5 | Belly of the Whale | the hero is swallowed by the unknown: a symbolic death |
| Initiation | 6 | The Road of Trials | a series of tests, often in threes |
| | 7 | The Meeting with the Goddess | an encounter with unconditional love or totality |
| | 8 | Woman as the Temptress | temptation away from the quest (Campbell's gendered label; not necessarily a woman) |
| | 9 | Atonement with the Father | confrontation with the ultimate power in the hero's life |
| | 10 | Apotheosis | a god-like expansion of understanding |
| | 11 | The Ultimate Boon | achieving the goal of the quest |
| Return | 12 | Refusal of the Return | reluctance to go back to the ordinary world |
| | 13 | The Magic Flight | escape with the boon, sometimes pursued |
| | 14 | Rescue from Without | help from outside to get back |
| | 15 | The Crossing of the Return Threshold | integrating what was learned into ordinary life |
| | 16 | Master of the Two Worlds | balance between the inner and outer worlds |
| | 17 | Freedom to Live | freedom from fear of death; living in the moment |

### Vogler's 12 stages

| # | Stage | Act | Approx. position | Function |
| :- | :---- | :-- | :--------------- | :------- |
| 1 | Ordinary World | I | 0–10% | the hero's normal life, as contrast |
| 2 | Call to Adventure | I | ~10% | problem or challenge appears |
| 3 | Refusal of the Call | I | 10–15% | fear or reluctance; shows the risk |
| 4 | Meeting with the Mentor | I | 15–20% | advice, training, a gift, confidence |
| 5 | Crossing the First Threshold | I → II | 20–25% | commits to the adventure; no going back |
| 6 | Tests, Allies, Enemies | II | 25–40% | learns the rules of the special world |
| 7 | Approach to the Inmost Cave | II | 40–50% | preparation for the central ordeal |
| 8 | The Ordeal | II | ~50% | the central crisis; a brush with death |
| 9 | Reward (Seizing the Sword) | II | 50–60% | takes possession of the treasure or knowledge |
| 10 | The Road Back | II → III | 70–80% | consequences; pursuit; recommitment |
| 11 | The Resurrection | III | 80–95% | a final, purifying test; the hero is transformed |
| 12 | Return with the Elixir | III | 95–100% | home with something that heals the ordinary world |

Criticisms worth knowing: folklorists argue Campbell selected the myths that fit and ignored those that did not; the
pattern is built around a male hero; and applied rigidly it gives every blockbuster the same shape. It fits quest and
coming-of-age stories best, and mysteries, tragedies and ensemble stories worst.

### Worked example: Star Wars (1977) on Vogler's stages

The canonical mapping, discussed by Vogler and many others. Positions are approximate.

| Stage | Star Wars (1977) |
| :---- | :--------------- |
| Ordinary World | Luke works his uncle's moisture farm on Tatooine and wants to leave for the Academy |
| Call to Adventure | a hidden message from Princess Leia in R2-D2, asking for Obi-Wan Kenobi's help |
| Refusal of the Call | Luke says he cannot go: he is needed for the harvest |
| Meeting with the Mentor | Obi-Wan tells him about his father and the Force and gives him a lightsaber |
| Crossing the First Threshold | Luke finds his aunt and uncle killed by Imperial troops, commits, and goes to Mos Eisley |
| Tests, Allies, Enemies | the cantina; hiring Han Solo and Chewbacca; escaping Imperial troops |
| Approach to the Inmost Cave | the *Millennium Falcon* is pulled into the Death Star |
| The Ordeal | rescuing Leia; the trash compactor; Obi-Wan's death |
| Reward | escape with Leia and the Death Star plans |
| The Road Back | the TIE fighter chase; the Rebels plan the attack as the Death Star approaches Yavin |
| The Resurrection | the trench run: Luke turns off his targeting computer, trusts the Force, and destroys the Death Star |
| Return with the Elixir | the medal ceremony; the Rebellion survives and Luke has a new identity |

What the example teaches: the Refusal is one line of dialogue, the Mentor stage takes a few scenes, and the Ordeal is
not the climax. Stages are functions, not chapters.

## Story Circle, seven-point structure and kishōtenketsu

### Dan Harmon's Story Circle

Dan Harmon (*Community*, *Rick and Morty*) simplified Campbell into eight steps on a circle, first posted as tutorials for
his Channel 101 short-film festival. The top half is the familiar world, the bottom half the unfamiliar; the story goes
down into the unknown and comes back.

| # | Keyword | Harmon's step | Clock position |
| :- | :------ | :------------ | :------------- |
| 1 | You | a character is in a zone of comfort | 0% |
| 2 | Need | but they want something | ~12% |
| 3 | Go | they enter an unfamiliar situation | ~25% |
| 4 | Search | adapt to it | ~37% |
| 5 | Find | get what they wanted | ~50% |
| 6 | Take | pay a heavy price for it | ~62% |
| 7 | Return | then return to their familiar situation | ~75% |
| 8 | Change | having changed | ~87–100% |

The circle is compact enough to use for one episode, one chapter or one scene. It is Harmon's tool for TV, where every
episode has to close its loop.

### Dan Wells's seven-point structure

Dan Wells (*I Am Not a Serial Killer*) popularised seven points in lectures at the Life, the Universe and Everything
(LTUE) conference in Utah; he credits the idea to the *Star Trek Roleplaying Game* narrator's guide (Decipher, 2002).
Design the **resolution first**, then the hook as its opposite, then the middle.

| # | Point | Function | Typical position |
| :- | :---- | :------- | :--------------- |
| 1 | Hook | the starting state, opposite of the resolution | 0% |
| 2 | Plot Turn 1 | the call to adventure: moves the story from the hook to the midpoint | ~20–25% |
| 3 | Pinch 1 | apply pressure: the antagonist shows up; forces action | ~35% |
| 4 | Midpoint | the character moves from reaction to action | ~50% |
| 5 | Pinch 2 | more pressure: the plan fails, the mentor dies; all seems lost | ~65% |
| 6 | Plot Turn 2 | the final piece: the character gets what they need to win | ~75–80% |
| 7 | Resolution | the climax; everything built toward this | ~90–100% |

Wells applies the seven points to each subplot separately, then interleaves them.

### Kishōtenketsu

A four-part structure used in Chinese, Korean and Japanese writing, from the Chinese *qǐ chéng zhuǎn hé* of classical
four-line (*jueju*) poetry. It is the standard shape of four-panel (*yonkoma*) comics.

| Part | Japanese | Function | Example (my own) |
| :--- | :------- | :------- | :--------------- |
| 1 | *ki* (起) introduction | characters, setting | an old woman feeds pigeons in a square every morning |
| 2 | *shō* (承) development | develops the situation, no turn yet | she names each bird and knows which ones are missing |
| 3 | *ten* (転) twist | an unexpected, apparently unrelated element | across town, a boy trains the same pigeons to carry notes |
| 4 | *ketsu* (結) conclusion | reconciles the parts into a new whole | one morning a pigeon lands with a note addressed to her |

Kishōtenketsu is often described in English as "structure without conflict". That overstates it: the structure does not
*require* a central conflict because the turn comes from juxtaposition, but stories built this way often contain
conflict. Its use for Western writers: slice-of-life, literary and cosy stories, and scenes whose payoff is a
recognition rather than a victory.

## MICE quotient

Orson Scott Card introduced the MICE quotient in *Characters & Viewpoint* (1988) and used it again in *How to Write
Science Fiction and Fantasy* (1990): every story mixes four elements, and the dominant one decides where the story
should start and end. Mary Robinette Kowal (on the *Writing Excuses* podcast and in her classes) renamed Idea to
**Inquiry** and turned it into a nesting tool.

| Thread | Starts when… | Ends when… | Example |
| :----- | :----------- | :--------- | :------ |
| **M**ilieu | a character enters a place | the character leaves it (or decides to stay) | *Gulliver's Travels*, *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland* |
| **I**dea / Inquiry | a question is raised | the question is answered | any whodunnit: a body is found → the killer is named |
| **C**haracter | a character is dissatisfied with who they are or their role | they change, accept, or give up | *A Christmas Carol*: Scrooge's miserliness → his redemption |
| **E**vent | the world's order is disrupted | a new order is established (or the old restored, or it all fails) | *The War of the Worlds*: invasion → the invaders die |

**Nesting (Kowal)**: close threads in the reverse order you opened them, like brackets or HTML tags. If a story opens
Milieu, then Event, then Inquiry, it closes Inquiry, then Event, then Milieu. Closing an outer thread first drains
tension from the ones still open.

```text
[M  arrive in the city
  [E  the plague breaks out
    [I  who poisoned the wells?
    I]  the physician did it
  E]  the plague is contained
M]  leave the city, changed
```

Uses: diagnosing a beginning or ending that feels wrong (a Milieu story that ends in the middle of the journey), sizing
a story (each extra thread adds words: short stories usually have one or two threads, novels many), and deciding which
subplot to resolve first.

## Try/fail cycles

The protagonist tries to solve the problem and fails, several times, each attempt costing more. Algis Budrys taught a
seven-part story shape in *Writing to the Point* (1994): a **character**, in a **context**, with a **problem**, makes
intelligent **attempts** that **fail** (usually three, escalating), then a final do-or-die attempt that brings
**victory** (or death), then **validation**, a moment confirming the story is over. The pattern spread
through David Farland, a Budrys student, to writers he taught, including Brandon Sanderson and Dan Wells.

Mary Robinette Kowal popularised the **"yes, but / no, and"** outcomes on *Writing Excuses*:

| Outcome of an attempt | Effect | Example |
| :-------------------- | :----- | :------ |
| **Yes, but** | the character succeeds, with a complication | she gets the ring back, **but** the buyer now knows who she is |
| **No, and** | the character fails, and it gets worse | she cannot get the ring back, **and** the city guard arrests her for theft |
| Yes | success; the thread closes | use only at the end of a thread |
| No | failure with no change; a dead scene | rewrite: add the "and" |

Rules of thumb (common workshop advice, not law): the number of failures before the final success signals how big the
problem is; the final attempt should use something the character learned from the failures; and a success that comes
without earlier failure feels unearned.

## Promises, progress, payoff

Brandon Sanderson's BYU creative-writing lectures (recorded in several years, including 2020 and 2025) frame plot as
three P's.

| Element | What it is | How to check it |
| :------ | :--------- | :-------------- |
| **Promises** | what the opening signals: genre, tone, the kind of problem, the kind of ending | would the first chapter's reader want *this* ending? |
| **Progress** | the reader's sense that the story is moving toward the promise, scene by scene | can you name what advanced in every chapter? |
| **Payoff** | the fulfilment of the promises | is every major promise kept, or deliberately and satisfyingly subverted? |

Practical consequences:

- The plot is often defined by the **type of progress** (a mystery progresses by clues, a romance by stages of a
  relationship, a heist by assembling the crew and plan, a travelogue by distance). Pick the type, then show a
  measurable step in each scene.
- **Promise X, deliver Y** works only if Y is better and the progress foreshadowed it. A comedy that turns into a
  tragedy with no warning breaks the promise.
- Early tone is a promise. An opening murder promises a mystery or thriller; an opening joke promises humour.
- In speculative fiction, rules are promises. A problem solved by magic the reader never saw breaks the contract (see
  Sanderson's laws on [magic and technology](/writing/worldbuilding/magic-technology/)).

## Subplots and B-stories

A **subplot** is a secondary line of action with its own goal and outcome. The **B story** (Snyder) is the subplot that
carries the theme, usually a relationship.

| Subplot job | Example |
| :---------- | :------ |
| carries the theme | the B-story relationship teaches the lesson the A story tests |
| complicates the main plot | the love interest is the antagonist's sister |
| varies pace and tone | a comic subplot between tense chapters |
| hides clues | a mystery's solution sits in the "unimportant" subplot |
| gives secondary characters arcs | an ally's own want and failure |
| shows the theme from another angle | a minor character makes the choice the hero refuses |

Rules: every subplot should **converge** on the climax (or be cut); resolve subplots just before the main plot, not after
(nesting again); a subplot needs at least three touches (set-up, development, payoff); and if a subplot is more
interesting than the main plot, consider swapping them.

**Braiding**: in multi-POV novels, interleave threads so that each chapter ends with a question and the reader is
switched away at a high point. Keep each thread moving at least every few chapters.

## Length and pacing across a story

### Length categories

SFWA (Nebula Awards) and the Hugo Awards use the same word-count bands. They are award rules, but magazines and agents use
the same vocabulary.

| Form | Words (Hugo/Nebula) | Typical threads (rough guide) | Typical scenes (rough guide) |
| :--- | :------------------ | :-------------- | :------------- |
| Flash fiction | under ~1,000 (common market usage; not a Hugo category) | one | 1–3 |
| Short story | under 7,500 | one, maybe two | 1–8 |
| Novelette | 7,500–17,500 | two or three | 6–15 |
| Novella | 17,500–40,000 | a main plot and a subplot or two | 15–35 |
| Novel | 40,000 and over | a main plot and several subplots | 40–100+ |

Commercial expectations are different: agents commonly quote adult debut novels at roughly 80,000–100,000 words and
adult SFF at roughly 90,000–120,000. Treat these as market rules of thumb that shift with genre and era.

### Proportions

| Rule of thumb | Why |
| :------------ | :-- |
| inciting incident within the first 10–15% | readers stop waiting for a story to start |
| act one ends by 20–25% | the protagonist must commit before the reader's patience runs out |
| something changes at the midpoint | stops the second act becoming a list of obstacles |
| the low point around 75% | leaves room for recovery and climax |
| climax and resolution in the last 10–20% | too short feels rushed; too long feels like an epilogue |
| the denouement is short | once the dramatic question is answered, the reader's interest drops fast |

Scene count is arithmetic: a 90,000-word novel with scenes of 1,500–3,000 words has roughly 30–60 scenes. If a
chapter-by-chapter outline has 25 scenes for a 100,000-word target, either scenes will bloat or the story is short.

### Pacing tools at the structural level

| Tool | Speeds up | Slows down |
| :--- | :-------- | :--------- |
| Scene length | short scenes | long scenes |
| Scene vs sequel ratio (see [scenes and prose](/writing/fiction/scene-prose/)) | more scenes (action) | more sequels (reaction, reflection) |
| Summary | skip time between scenes | — |
| Ticking clock | a deadline in the second half | — |
| Cross-cutting | switching between threads at high points | switching at low points |
| Chapter endings | end on a question or turn | end on resolution |
| Stakes | raise them at each turn | keep them constant |

## Outlining vs discovery writing

| Approach | Also called | Strengths | Risks | Fixes |
| :------- | :---------- | :-------- | :---- | :---- |
| **Outlining** | plotter; "architect" | structure, foreshadowing and endings work first time; faster drafting | characters serve the plot; story feels mechanical; boredom with a solved book | leave scenes loose; outline goals not events; revise the outline as you go |
| **Discovery writing** | pantser (from "flying by the seat of your pants"); "gardener" | organic characters, surprise, voice | saggy middles; abandoned drafts; endings that do not pay off; heavy revision | reverse-outline after the draft; know the ending before the last third |
| Hybrid | "plantser" | most working writers | — | outline the tent poles (inciting incident, midpoint, climax), discover the rest |

George R. R. Martin describes two kinds of writers in several interviews: **architects**, who draw the blueprints before
they build, and **gardeners**, who plant a seed and see what grows; he calls himself mostly a gardener. Stephen King's
*On Writing* (2000) describes starting from a situation rather than a plot. Sanderson describes outlining the plot and
discovery-writing the characters.

Neither method is better. What matters is that the final draft has structure, however it got there. Discovery writers
do their structural work in revision; outliners do it before drafting.

**Reverse outline** (for discovery writers and revisions): after a draft, list each scene in one line with its POV,
goal, turn and word count. Structural problems (a missing midpoint, a 20,000-word stretch with no turn) show up at once.

## Structural problems and fixes

| Problem | Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| :------ | :------ | :---- | :-- |
| Saggy middle | readers stall between 30% and 70% | obstacles repeat without escalating; no midpoint | add a midpoint reversal; escalate stakes; add a ticking clock; give the protagonist a plan that fails |
| Slow start | first chapters are backstory and setup | writer warming up | start at the inciting incident or just before; feed backstory in later |
| Deus ex machina | the climax is solved by coincidence, a new power or a rescuer | the writer painted the character into a corner | set up the solution earlier; make the protagonist cause the win; coincidence may cause trouble, never end it |
| Unearned ending | the character changes at the climax without cause | the arc was not dramatised | seed the lie and the lesson in act one; show failure from the old way in act two |
| Passive protagonist | things happen *to* the hero | goals too vague; antagonist does all the choosing | give the hero a concrete goal and a plan in every act; the hero makes the climactic choice |
| Episodic plot | chapters can be reordered with no loss | "and then" joins | apply the therefore/but test; make each event cause the next |
| Stakes plateau | the climax is no harder than the first obstacle | no escalation plan | list obstacles in order of difficulty and personal cost |
| Broken promise | the ending is a different genre from the opening | promise not tracked | list the opening's promises and tick them off in the last act |
| Two climaxes | a second ending after the real one | subplot closed after main plot | nest: close subplots before the main plot |
| Dangling threads | questions never answered | discovery drafting | reverse-outline; resolve or cut every opened thread |
| Info-dump prologue | pages of history before the story | worldbuilding love | put the history where a character needs it (see [worldbuilding fundamentals](/writing/worldbuilding/fundamentals/)) |
| Villain-driven plot | the antagonist makes every choice | protagonist reactive | the protagonist must change from reactive to proactive by the midpoint |

## When the models do not apply

- **Literary and experimental fiction** often values pattern, voice and image over causal plot. Structure still exists
  (repetition, juxtaposition, a change in understanding) but the beat sheets do not describe it.
- **Short fiction** has room for one turn. Most short stories are a single scene or a try/fail cycle with a twist, not a
  compressed three-act film.
- **Ensembles and epics** run several structures in parallel. Map each POV's arc separately, then braid.
- **Tragedy** inverts the payoff: the character fails because of who they are. Freytag describes it better than Snyder.
- **Series** need both an episode structure and a series arc. The Story Circle suits episodes; the series arc can be any
  of the larger models.
- **Games and interactive fiction** hand some choices to the player; see [game worlds](/writing/worldbuilding/game-worlds/).

### Exercises

1. Take a film you know well and write its beats as one sentence each. Mark every join as "and then", "therefore" or
   "but". Then do the same for your own outline.
2. Map a favourite novel onto the Save the Cat! beats using page numbers. Note where it deviates and why it still works.
3. Write the same premise as a three-act outline and as a kishōtenketsu outline. Which one suits it?
4. List the MICE threads your current story opens, in order. Check that they close in reverse order.
5. For your protagonist's central problem, write three escalating attempts, each ending "yes, but" or "no, and".
6. Write the resolution first, then a hook that is its opposite (Wells's method).
