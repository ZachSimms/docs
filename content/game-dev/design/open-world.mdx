## What "open world" means

An open world is a continuous space the player can **approach freely**: pick the order, the route and often the
goal. Most games sit somewhere between fully linear and fully open.

| Shape | What it is | Examples | Choose it when |
| :---- | :--------- | :------- | :------------- |
| **Open world** | one continuous map, most of it reachable early; systems keep running everywhere | *The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild*, *Elden Ring*, *Skyrim* | exploration and self-set goals are the core fantasy |
| **Open zones** | several large open regions, reached from a menu, a hub or in sequence | *Dragon Age: Inquisition*, *Metal Gear Solid V: The Phantom Pain* (Afghanistan, Central Africa) | you want freedom inside a region but control over the order of regions |
| **Wide-linear** | a linear sequence of spaces, some of them wide open sandboxes | *Metro Exodus*, *Uncharted: The Lost Legacy* (the Western Ghats) | authored pacing matters, with bursts of freedom |
| **Hub** | a home base with branches to levels or missions | *Super Mario Odyssey* (the Odyssey and its kingdoms) | clear structure, natural place for story and upgrades |
| **Interconnected / metroidvania** | one connected map gated by abilities, with shortcuts | *Hollow Knight*, *Dark Souls* | mastery of one place and satisfying loops back |

The table of all structures, with trade-offs, is in
[world structures](/writing/worldbuilding/game-worlds/#world-structures).

### When not to go open world

| Warning sign | Why it hurts | Instead |
| :----------- | :----------- | :------ |
| The story needs urgency or a fixed order | the player can wander for 50 hours while "the world ends tonight" | linear or wide-linear; a hub between chapters |
| Small team, short schedule | an open world multiplies content, testing and technical work | a dense hub or a few open zones |
| Core mechanics need controlled spaces | precision platforming, puzzle sequences, tuned encounters | authored levels; open only between them |
| No verb makes the space itself fun | crossing the map becomes a chore between activities | shrink the map or build traversal first |
| "Open world" is a marketing box to tick | size without purpose reads as emptiness | pick the shape that serves the core loop |

## Design pillars

Three pillars recur across open-world talks and interviews. Each design decision should serve at least one.

| Pillar | Meaning | Test | Real examples |
| :----- | :------ | :--- | :------------ |
| **Freedom** | the player chooses order, route and approach | can the player skip, reorder or solve this another way? | *Breath of the Wild* lets the player head for the final boss once the opening plateau is done; Hidetaka Miyazaki said the aim for *Elden Ring* was for players to "enjoy the game in their own way and feel liberated in this new open world" |
| **Discovery** | the world rewards curiosity with things the player finds, not things the UI points at | would a curious player find this without a marker? | *Outer Wilds* has no upgrades or collectibles: knowledge is the only progress |
| **Emergence** | systems interact to produce situations nobody scripted | does this rule combine with others? | the *Breath of the Wild* "chemistry engine" (see [systemic design](#systemic-design)); Dan Hay described *Far Cry 5* as an "anecdote factory" |

Freedom without discovery is an empty map; discovery without freedom is a museum; emergence without the other two is
a toy box. Write the pillars down in one line each and use them to cut features.

## Landmarks and sightlines

Players navigate by what they can see. The world's large shapes are its interface.

### The triangle rule

At CEDEC 2017, *Breath of the Wild* director Hidemaro Fujibayashi and terrain lead artist Makoto Yonezu presented the
game's field level design, including what the team called the **triangle rule**: build terrain from triangular
shapes (mountains, hills, rock spires).

<Diagram src="/images/diagrams/open-world-triangle-rule.svg" label="A side view of terrain seen by a player standing on the left. A small, shallow hill is closest. Behind it a medium triangle hides the lower part of a tall landmark tower, so only the tower's top shows above the ridge; a dashed sightline runs from the player's eye to the tower top. An arrow up the medium triangle's slope is labeled climb, and a dashed curved arrow at its base is labeled or go around. At the right a large triangle is labeled large: seen from afar, sets a goal. A note reads: each ridge hides what lies beyond it: see a peak, choose to climb or go around, and find the rest on the way." caption="The triangle rule: every ridge is a choice and a reveal" />

| What a triangle does | How | Design use |
| :------------------- | :-- | :--------- |
| **Offers a choice** | climb over it or walk around it | two routes from every obstacle, both leading somewhere |
| **Hides what is behind it** | the far side stays unseen until the player commits | surprise: a camp, a shrine, a view; landmarks appear bit by bit |
| **Draws the eye** | the peak is a natural focal point | put a reward (or a lure) on or near the top |
| **Works at every scale** | large (seen from afar), medium (a climb), small (a bump) | Robert Yang's summary stresses clear contrast between the three sizes, used everywhere |

### Gravity and height

The same talk described **gravity** (引力): places that pull the player in, the way light attracts insects
(Fujibayashi's image, as reported by Famitsu). Shrines were made visible from far off, stables offered information,
enemy camps held useful weapons, strange rock formations invited a closer look. Height signals importance: the taller
a structure, the more it matters.

| Tool | Guideline |
| :--- | :-------- |
| **Global landmarks** | a few shapes visible from most of the map: a castle, a volcano, a giant tree. They orient ("the castle is north") |
| **Local landmarks** | one per region, visible within it: a lighthouse, a ruined arch |
| **Silhouettes** | each landmark needs a shape readable at a distance and in fog; no two alike |
| **Verticality** | high points are vantage points: reward the climb with a view of three or more new points of interest |
| **Lures** | smoke, light, birds, sound, a glint of loot: small gravity between big landmarks |
| **Occlusion** | ridges, forests and buildings hide as much as they show; a fully visible map has no surprises |

Weenies, sightlines in towns and Lynch's elements are on
[game world design](/writing/worldbuilding/game-worlds/#spatial-design-making-spaces-legible).

## Density and pacing

Density is **time between interesting things**, not things per square kilometer. It depends on movement speed,
sightlines and what counts as interesting.

<Diagram src="/images/diagrams/open-world-activity-loop.svg" label="A loop of five stages drawn as a circle of arrows. 1 Spot: a landmark, smoke or an odd shape on the horizon. 2 Travel: movement is play. 3 Detour: gravity, a nearer pull. 4 Arrive: loot, lore or a skill. 5 Vantage: climb up, see more, which leads back to Spot. The center reads: the loop, each stop shows the next." caption="The open-world activity loop" />

| Layer | Spacing guideline (rule of thumb) | Examples (invented) |
| :---- | :-------------------------------- | :------------------ |
| **Major** (story, dungeon, town) | one per region; visible from far away | a fortress city, a volcano temple |
| **Minor** (camp, puzzle, side quest) | the next one visible or audible from the last | a bandit camp, a sealed door, a stranded merchant |
| **Micro** (pickup, vignette, view) | fills the travel between minor points | a rare herb, a skeleton with a note, a waterfall view |
| **Rest** (empty on purpose) | after intense stretches; before reveals | a quiet plain, a long valley with music |

Rules:

- **Something is always in view.** From anywhere, the player should see at least one thing they haven't done.
- **Vary the rewards.** Loot, lore, a new ability, a shortcut, a view, a story: the same reward everywhere feels like a chore.
- **Vary the activities.** A map of 200 identical camps is one activity repeated, not 200.
- **Leave emptiness deliberately.** Quiet stretches create anticipation and make the next find matter.

### Checklist fatigue and the map-reveal tower

The **tower** that reveals a region's map and fills it with icons became known as the "Ubisoft tower", after the
*Assassin's Creed* series popularized it. It solves onboarding but turns the world into a list: the player stops
looking at the world and starts clearing icons.

| Approach | Example | Effect |
| :------- | :------ | :----- |
| Tower reveals icons | *Assassin's Creed*, *Far Cry 3* (radio towers), *Far Cry 4* (bell towers) | clear goals; checklist feel |
| No towers; people and exploration reveal content | *Far Cry 5* dropped towers for discovery through characters | discovery through play |
| Tower reveals terrain only | *Breath of the Wild* towers show the land, not the points of interest | the player still has to look |
| Map fragments, player-placed markers | *Elden Ring* map fragments show terrain; players mark what they find | the map is the player's notes |

If you keep a reveal mechanic, reveal **terrain and names**, not every activity, and let the player place their own
markers.

## Traversal

If moving is fun, the world between points of interest is content; if it isn't, the world is a loading screen.

| Traversal | Makes the world | Watch out for |
| :-------- | :-------------- | :------------ |
| **Climbing anything** (*Breath of the Wild*) | every cliff is a route; the triangle rule works | a stamina limit or weather is needed to keep choices meaningful |
| **Gliding** | height becomes distance; vantage points become launch points | flattens the map if too cheap |
| **Mounts** (*Elden Ring*'s Torrent drew on *Sekiro*'s traversal, Miyazaki said) | big distances feel short; mounted combat | terrain the mount can't handle; summoning rules |
| **Vehicles** | speed and spectacle | the world must be built for the speed: roads, sightlines, streaming |
| **Grapple, swing, wall-run** (*Marvel's Spider-Man*) | a city becomes a playground | needs anchor points everywhere; hard to gate |
| **Walking as the game** (*Death Stranding*) | terrain itself is the challenge | only works if the whole design is built around it |

### Fast travel

| Choice | Pros | Cons |
| :----- | :--- | :--- |
| Anywhere, from the start | no tedium | the world between points stops existing |
| To discovered points only | exploration first, convenience after | backtracking is still quick |
| From in-world places only (stables, stations) | fiction and friction | can feel arbitrary |
| Costs something (time, currency, supplies) | a real decision | punishes players with less time |
| None | the journey is the game | only if traversal is excellent and the map is dense |

Most games unlock fast travel after discovery and tie it to a place in the world.

## Guidance without hand-holding

Guidance ranges from a UI arrow to nothing at all. Move toward the world end of this scale wherever playtests allow.

| Level | Technique | Example |
| :---- | :-------- | :------ |
| **Explicit UI** | quest marker, route line, compass pip | most open worlds; reliable and accessible |
| **Directions** | written or spoken directions: "east of the river, past the broken bridge" | *The Elder Scrolls III: Morrowind*'s journal |
| **Diegetic pointers** | something in the world points the way | *Ghost of Tsushima*'s Guiding Wind blows toward the goal instead of a map marker; *Elden Ring*'s Sites of Grace can show golden rays toward the main path |
| **Landmarks and gravity** | the player sees something and goes | the triangle rule, towers, smoke |
| **Knowledge** | the player works out where to go from clues | *Outer Wilds*: the ship's log records rumors to follow up |

Rules:

- **Give directions that can be followed in the world.** "North of the lake" needs a lake the player can find.
- **Record what the player learned.** A journal, map notes or a log replaces the marker without losing the player.
- **Offer a fallback.** An optional marker or hint system keeps less experienced players playing; default it off only if playtests show most players manage.
- **Don't let UI replace the world.** If players stare at the minimap, the landmarks aren't doing their job.

The "yellow paint" debate and general guidance cues (light, color, leading lines) are on
[game world design](/writing/worldbuilding/game-worlds/#guiding-the-player).

## Progression and gating

Gates decide what the player can reach when. Soft gates keep freedom; hard gates keep order.

<Diagram src="/images/diagrams/open-world-gates.svg" label="A horizontal scale from soft, where the player decides when, to hard, where the designer decides when. Four gate types sit on it from left to right. Difficulty: strong enemies, cold, heat or storms; skill or gear gets you through early; example Elden Ring areas. Knowledge: a fact you learn such as a route, a timing or a password, no item to find; example Outer Wilds. Ability: a tool or a move such as a glider, grapple, swimming or a key; show the gate first; example metroidvanias. Story: locked until a plot beat happens; keeps order but costs freedom, so use few; example most story games. A note reads: most open worlds mix all four; the further right, the more the order of play is fixed." caption="Four kinds of gate, from soft to hard" />

| Gate | Player experience | Design notes |
| :--- | :---------------- | :----------- |
| **Difficulty** (soft) | "I'm not ready yet, but I could try" | readable danger: enemy look, level shown, environmental hints; Miyazaki said players can come back later when they're at an impasse |
| **Environment** (soft) | cold, heat, poison, darkness | a consumable or gear counters it; skilled players push through |
| **Knowledge** | "now I understand" | the answer must be learnable in the world; log the clues |
| **Ability** | "I need something first" | show the gate before the key so the player remembers it |
| **Story** (hard) | "the game won't let me" | use for a few big beats; explain the lock in the fiction |

### Level scaling vs fixed zones

| Model | How | Pros | Cons |
| :---- | :-- | :--- | :--- |
| **Fixed zones** | each area has a set difficulty | the world has a shape; returning stronger feels good | players can wander into a wall |
| **Full scaling** | enemies match the player's level | any order works | growth feels pointless; *The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion*'s scaling was criticized because characters who leveled through non-combat skills fell behind their enemies |
| **Ranged zones** | a zone locks its level within a range when first entered | order flexible, growth still visible | tuning each range |
| **Scaled with caps** | enemies scale, but types stop at a ceiling | late-game power shows | ceilings must be tuned |

*Skyrim* combines the last two: dungeons have a level range, and enemy types stop getting stronger past a plateau
(UESP's leveling notes). Whatever the model, make the **danger readable** before the fight.

## Quests and content

| Kind | How it's made | Strength | Weakness |
| :--- | :------------ | :------- | :------- |
| **Handcrafted** | written, scripted and placed by hand | memorable stories, surprises, choices | expensive; limited count |
| **Radiant / templated** | a template filled from pools: location, target, reward | cheap, endless | *Skyrim*'s Radiant system "cannot create large, complicated, or particularly interesting quests" (UESP) |
| **Procedural** | generated from rules, often from world state | scale, replay | sameness ("procedural oatmeal") |
| **Systemic / emergent** | no quest: systems create a situation | the player's own stories | hard to direct or guarantee |

### Main and side content

| Content | Job | Guideline |
| :------ | :-- | :-------- |
| **Main quest** | the spine: motivation, stakes, a sense of an ending | short enough to finish; points into the world, not away from it |
| **Side quests** | character, place and theme | *The Witcher 3*'s quest team "declared war on 'fetch quests'" (Mateusz Tomaszkiewicz, 2016): every quest needs a story |
| **Activities** | repeatable play: races, hunts, camps, puzzles | vary them; tie rewards to progression |
| **Collectibles** | reasons to look closely | reward with lore or ability, not just a counter |

### Quest templates, made interesting

| Template | Flat version | Better version (invented) |
| :------- | :----------- | :------------------------ |
| Fetch | bring me 10 pelts | the pelts are from wolves that aren't wolves; the hunter who asked knows |
| Kill | clear the camp | the bandits are deserters with a reason; talk, sneak or fight |
| Escort | follow the slow NPC | the NPC takes a shortcut that shows you a new route |
| Investigate | follow the glowing clues | clues in the world, deduced by the player, recorded in the journal |
| Deliver | carry a letter | reading it changes who you give it to |

CD Projekt's Tomaszkiewicz, on *Cyberpunk 2077*'s quests (E3 2019): "We never fail the quest based on choices. We
treat those things as outcomes." Consequences keep the quest going instead of resetting it.

## Systemic design

At GDC 2017 ("Change and Constant"), Fujibayashi, art director Satoru Takizawa and technical director Takuhiro Dohta
described a shift from **additive** design (each object has one scripted use) to **multiplicative** design: objects
react to the player's actions, and to each other. The team prototyped these rules in a 2D top-down version of the
game, burning grass and cutting trees before the 3D world existed.

Dohta called the result a **chemistry engine**, a "rule-based state calculator", beside the physics engine. As
reported from the talk:

| Rule | Example |
| :--- | :------ |
| Elements (fire, water, ice, electricity, wind) can change a material's state | fire burns grass; electricity runs through metal |
| Elements can change each other's state | water puts out fire; fire melts ice |
| Materials don't change each other's state | a rock doesn't change a tree |

| Principle | Why | Example (invented unless noted) |
| :-------- | :-- | :------------------------------ |
| **Few rules, many objects** | each new object inherits every interaction | anything wooden burns, floats and can be cut |
| **Consistent** | players plan only with rules they trust | if one metal box conducts, all metal boxes conduct |
| **Visible** | a rule the player can't see isn't a tool | sparks on wet ground, smoke from burning grass |
| **Shared by player and enemies** | systems cut both ways and create stories | enemies' camps burn too; lightning hits enemies holding metal |
| **Designed puzzles have several answers** | the systems will find more anyway | a shrine that accepts any way to weigh down the plate |

Emergent situations need **testing tools**: spawn anything anywhere, record-and-replay, and a way to set weather and
time.

## Reactive world state

A world feels alive when it runs without the player and remembers what the player did.

| Kind | What changes | Examples |
| :--- | :----------- | :------- |
| **Schedules** | NPCs sleep, work, travel, eat | *Majora's Mask*'s three-day cycle, where each resident follows a timetable; *Oblivion*'s Radiant AI schedules |
| **Clocks** | events happen whether the player is there or not | *Outer Wilds*: "Everything is on its own clock," said Alex Beachum |
| **Factions** | territory, reputation, alliances | *Far Cry 5*'s Resistance meter builds per region toward story events |
| **Persistence** | the world keeps what the player did | burned villages stay burned; *Middle-earth: Shadow of Mordor*'s Nemesis system has orc captains remember encounters |
| **Ecology** | predators, prey, resources move and respawn | herds migrate; overhunting thins them (invented) |
| **Weather and time** | visibility, danger, access | fog hides landmarks; a river freezes at night (invented) |

Rules:

- **Show the change.** A reputation number nobody notices is wasted; guards who mention the fire are not.
- **Budget branches.** Every persistent change multiplies later content (dialogue, art states, tests).
- **Simulate near, fake far.** Full simulation near the player; coarse updates (or timestamps) far away.
- **Store changes, not the world.** Save what differs from the authored start (see [save state](#save-state)).

Faction design and reputation systems are on [societies and cultures](/writing/worldbuilding/societies-cultures/) and
[game world design](/writing/worldbuilding/game-worlds/#factions-reputation-and-reactive-worlds).

## Story in an open world

The **urgency problem**: the story says "hurry", the structure says "wander". Players either feel guilty exploring
or stop believing the story (the ludonarrative dissonance described on
[game world design](/writing/worldbuilding/game-worlds/#ludonarrative-dissonance)).

| Technique | How | Example |
| :-------- | :-- | :------ |
| **Stakes that wait** | the threat is contained, or the goal is personal and slow | *Breath of the Wild*: Zelda has held Ganon back for a century; the player chooses when to go |
| **A real clock** | time really runs out; the structure is built around repeats | *Majora's Mask*'s three days; *Outer Wilds*' loop |
| **Order-free story beats** | beats can be found in any order and still make sense | *Breath of the Wild*'s recovered memories |
| **Acts with open middles** | a linear opening and ending, open regions between | many open-world RPGs |
| **Reactive dialogue** | characters mention what the player did and in what order | flags checked in dialogue conditions |
| **Stories in places** | local stories finish where they start; the main story threads through them | side quests that are complete short stories |

Writing tips:

- Write each beat to stand alone: who, where, why now.
- Keep a **state table** of what the player may or may not know at each beat; test the odd orders.
- Let the player's choices echo later, cheaply: a line of dialogue, a changed sign, a new NPC in town.

## Technical considerations

A large world has to be streamed in, drawn cheaply at a distance, kept numerically stable far from the origin, and
saved without writing everything.

| Problem | Technique | Notes |
| :------ | :-------- | :---- |
| The world doesn't fit in memory | **streaming**: split the world into cells or chunks; load those near the player on background threads, unload far ones | stream ahead of the player in the direction of travel; faster traversal needs a bigger radius |
| Distant geometry costs too much | **LOD**: simpler meshes at distance; **HLOD**: one merged mesh replaces a group of objects far away | fewer draw calls; bake HLODs per cell |
| Hidden geometry is still drawn | **occlusion culling** | works best indoors and in dense cities; weak on open terrain |
| Jitter far from the origin | **origin shifting** (floating origin) or **double precision** | 32-bit floats lose precision as values grow |
| Saving a huge world | **delta saves**: store only what changed, keyed by stable ids per cell | load the authored cell, then apply its deltas |
| Hitches on arrival | preload, time-slice instancing, pool common objects | profile on the slowest target hardware |

### Floating point

A 32-bit float has 24 bits of precision, so the smallest step between values grows with distance from zero. Godot's
docs give these as the **maximum recommended single-precision ranges** (in units, usually meters) before rendering or
physics artifacts:

| Game type | Range |
| :-------- | :---- |
| First-person 3D | 2,048 to 4,096 |
| Third-person 3D | 4,096 to 8,192 |
| Top-down 3D | 16,384 to 32,768 |
| Any 3D game | 32,768 to 65,536; beyond that double precision is usually required |

| Fix | How | Cost |
| :-- | :-- | :--- |
| **Origin shifting** | when the player passes a threshold, move the whole world back so the player is near zero | touches every system that stores positions; tricky in multiplayer (Godot's docs say so) |
| **Double precision** | build the engine with doubles | custom engine build; slower; shaders still use 32-bit floats |
| **Smaller world** | keep the playable area within the ranges above | often the best answer |

### Save state

| Store | Example |
| :---- | :------ |
| Global flags | main quest stage, discovered regions, time of day |
| Per-cell deltas | `cell (3, -2): chest_07 opened, bandit_camp_2 cleared` |
| Per-entity state for important NPCs | position, schedule step, relationship |
| Respawn timestamps | "camp cleared at day 12" instead of simulating the camp |

Give every placed object a **stable id** in the editor, never an index that changes when a designer adds a crate.

### In Godot 4.7

| Need | Godot 4.7 | Notes |
| :--- | :-------- | :---- |
| Background loading | `ResourceLoader.load_threaded_request()`, `load_threaded_get_status()`, `load_threaded_get()` | details on [threaded loading](/game-dev/godot/resources-saving-export/#threaded-loading); `load_threaded_get()` blocks if the load isn't finished |
| Adding chunks | `add_child()` on the main thread | the active scene tree is not thread-safe; build off-tree, or use `call_deferred` from threads |
| Automatic LOD | mesh LOD is generated on import (glTF, `.blend`, FBX) | tune with `lod_bias` per instance and the viewport's `mesh_lod_threshold` |
| Hierarchical LOD | **visibility ranges** on `GeometryInstance3D`: `visibility_range_begin`, `visibility_range_end`, margins, fade mode, `visibility_parent` | a merged far mesh as the parent hides detailed children at distance |
| Occlusion culling | Project Settings > Rendering > Occlusion Culling > Use Occlusion Culling, plus a baked `OccluderInstance3D` | the docs recommend LOD and visibility ranges instead for open outdoor scenes |
| Large coordinates | engine compiled with `scons precision=double` | official builds are single precision; GDExtensions must be rebuilt |
| Terrain | a terrain add-on such as Terrain3D (a GDExtension), or chunked meshes | Godot has no built-in 3D terrain node |

A chunk streamer that keeps a square of chunk scenes around the player loaded:

```gdscript
# chunk_streamer.gd: keeps chunks near the player loaded
extends Node3D

const CHUNK_SIZE := 256.0   # meters per chunk side
const RADIUS := 2           # chunks kept in each direction
const PATH := "res://world/chunks/chunk_%d_%d.tscn"

@export var player: Node3D

var _loaded: Dictionary[Vector2i, Node3D] = {}
var _pending: Dictionary[Vector2i, String] = {}

func _process(_delta: float) -> void:
    var wanted := _wanted(_chunk_of(player.global_position))
    for coord in wanted:
        if not (_loaded.has(coord) or _pending.has(coord)):
            _request(coord)
    _poll()
    for coord in _loaded.keys():
        if not wanted.has(coord):
            _loaded[coord].queue_free()
            _loaded.erase(coord)

func _chunk_of(pos: Vector3) -> Vector2i:
    return Vector2i(floori(pos.x / CHUNK_SIZE),
        floori(pos.z / CHUNK_SIZE))

func _wanted(center: Vector2i) -> Array[Vector2i]:
    var out: Array[Vector2i] = []
    for x in range(-RADIUS, RADIUS + 1):
        for z in range(-RADIUS, RADIUS + 1):
            out.append(center + Vector2i(x, z))
    return out

func _request(coord: Vector2i) -> void:
    var path := PATH % [coord.x, coord.y]
    if not ResourceLoader.exists(path):
        return                  # sea or map edge: no chunk
    ResourceLoader.load_threaded_request(path)
    _pending[coord] = path

func _poll() -> void:
    for coord in _pending.keys():
        var path: String = _pending[coord]
        match ResourceLoader.load_threaded_get_status(path):
            ResourceLoader.THREAD_LOAD_LOADED:
                _pending.erase(coord)
                var scene: PackedScene = \
                    ResourceLoader.load_threaded_get(path)
                var chunk := scene.instantiate() as Node3D
                chunk.position = Vector3(coord.x, 0.0,
                    coord.y) * CHUNK_SIZE
                add_child(chunk)   # main thread: safe
                _loaded[coord] = chunk
            ResourceLoader.THREAD_LOAD_FAILED, \
            ResourceLoader.THREAD_LOAD_INVALID_RESOURCE:
                _pending.erase(coord)
                push_error("chunk failed: " + path)
```

Next steps for a real game: unload with a bigger radius than you load (hysteresis) so chunks don't flicker at a
border, instance at most one chunk per frame, apply each chunk's save deltas right after `instantiate()`, and give
distant chunks a low-detail stand-in with visibility ranges.

## Scope and production

An open world multiplies everything: content, bugs, test time, streaming budgets. Plan for it.

| Practice | Why |
| :------- | :-- |
| **Vertical slice first** | one finished region proves the loop, density, traversal and tech before the map grows |
| **Content budget per region** | count majors, minors, micros and rests per region; track them like money |
| **Size from travel time** | set the map size from the minutes it takes to cross at each traversal speed, not from square kilometers |
| **Tools before content** | placement, scatter, road and river tools, quest editors, debug teleport and state editing |
| **Modular kits** | reusable building and rock pieces with a few hero assets per region |
| **Reuse with variation** | the same camp layout with a new faction, weather, time or twist |
| **Guard quest dependencies** | Pawel Sasko on *The Witcher 3*: "any change of asset, location or scene may cause some unexpected consequences in the quest" |
| **Cut regions, not density** | a smaller full map beats a larger empty one |

## Playtesting and telemetry

| Method | Reveals | Real use |
| :----- | :------ | :------- |
| **Position heatmaps** | where players go and don't | *Breath of the Wild*'s early playtest heatmaps showed players sticking to roads; later ones showed paths spread across the map |
| **Path traces** | the routes between points, backtracking, circling | spot confusing areas and unused routes |
| **Death and stuck maps** | difficulty spikes, bad collision, soft locks | Bungie tracked where *Halo 3* testers died, "down to the square foot" (Wired, 2007) |
| **Time between discoveries** | whether density holds up | long gaps mean empty stretches or bad sightlines |
| **Marker and map usage** | whether landmarks work | frequent map opening means the world isn't readable |
| **Order of completion** | whether the story holds in odd orders | test the orders players actually take |
| **Silent observation, think-aloud** | why the numbers look the way they do | telemetry shows what, watching shows why |

Log events with a position, time, and player id: `discovered`, `died`, `quest_step`, `fast_travel`, `map_opened`.
Plot them over the map image; compare builds.

## Common mistakes

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| :------ | :---- | :-- |
| "It's big but empty" | map sized before content; density by area | shrink the map; space content by travel time |
| Players follow the minimap, not the world | markers do all the guiding | landmarks, directions, diegetic cues; markers optional |
| A map full of icons | reveal mechanic shows every activity | reveal terrain only; let players place markers |
| Every camp feels the same | one template repeated | vary layout, faction, twist and reward |
| Players get lost or quit early | no global landmarks; unreadable danger | global landmarks; readable gates; a guidance fallback |
| Levels feel pointless | full level scaling | fixed or ranged zones; visible growth |
| Story urgency rings false | "hurry" plus a map to wander | stakes that wait, a real clock, or order-free beats |
| Systems nobody uses | rules hidden or inconsistent | show the rules in play; teach them once, safely |
| Traversal is a chore | walking between points is the only verb | movement as play; shortcuts; better fast travel |
| Pop-in and hitches | streaming too late, too much per frame | bigger radius, time-slicing, LOD and HLOD |
| Jitter far from spawn | 32-bit floats far from the origin | smaller map, origin shifting or double precision |
| Saves break after patches | saves keyed by index or scene order | stable ids; per-cell deltas; versioned saves |
| Quests break in odd orders | untested states | state tables; automated order tests |

GLOBAL LANDMARKS (visible from most of the map):

TRAVERSAL VERBS:    fast travel rule:
GUIDANCE: markers? directions? diegetic cue? journal?
