A survey of the major leadership theories, from traits to transformational, servant, Level 5, adaptive and
authentic leadership, plus power, trust, influence and followership, each with a blunt rating of how good the
evidence is. Practical management techniques (one-on-ones, delegation, feedback, hiring) are in
[people management](/leadership/management/), team-level models in
[five dysfunctions](/leadership/five-dysfunctions/), and the military-derived models in
[OODA loop](/leadership/ooda-loop/), [extreme ownership](/leadership/extreme-ownership/) and
[mission command](/leadership/mission-command/).

## How to read the evidence ratings

Leadership research is noisy: most studies are cross-sectional surveys in which followers rate their own leader on
both the "style" and the outcomes, which inflates correlations. Popular books are mostly case studies picked after
the fact. Ratings used on this sheet:

| Rating | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **A** | strong: multiple meta-analyses, replicated, some longitudinal or experimental evidence |
| **B** | moderate: meta-analytic support, but measurement or causality problems |
| **C** | weak or mixed: little independent testing, or tests found limited support |
| **D** | little or none: practitioner framework, anecdote, retrospective case selection, or claims that are contradicted |

No major leadership model earns a clean A. The closest are the trait and behaviour meta-analyses, and the
psychological safety research in [five dysfunctions](/leadership/five-dysfunctions/#what-the-evidence-says-complementary-models).

## Trait approaches

**Idea**: leaders are born with, or at least have, distinctive traits. Thomas Carlyle's 19th-century "great man" view
is the ancestor.

| Period | What happened |
| :--- | :--- |
| up to the 1940s | researchers searched for the universal traits of leaders |
| 1948 | Ralph Stogdill's review (*Journal of Psychology*) found no consistent trait profile across situations; traits mattered, but only in relation to the situation. It was widely read as the end of trait theory. |
| 1950s–1980s | the field moved to behaviours (what leaders do) and contingency models (what works when) |
| 1986 onward | meta-analyses revived traits: with better methods and the Big Five, some traits consistently predict leadership |

| Finding | Source |
| :--- | :--- |
| Big Five correlations with leadership: extraversion .31, conscientiousness .28, openness .24, neuroticism −.24, agreeableness .08; multiple R = .48 | Judge, Bono, Ilies and Gerhardt 2002, *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 73 samples |
| extraversion was the most consistent correlate, for both **emerging** as a leader and being rated **effective** | same |
| intelligence and leadership: .21 uncorrected, .27 corrected, lower than previously thought | Judge, Colbert and Ilies 2004, 151 samples |

**Why traits fell out of favour and partly returned.** Early work lacked a common trait taxonomy and mixed up
emerging as a leader (being seen as one) with being effective. The Big Five gave a common language, and meta-analysis
showed small-to-moderate but reliable effects. Traits predict who gets picked and, less strongly, who does well; they
don't tell you what to do.

**Evidence: B.** Useful for selection and self-awareness. Caveat: extraversion predicts being *seen* as a leader more
than being effective, so selection on charisma tends to over-promote confident talkers.

### Behaviour and contingency models, briefly

| Model | Core idea | Evidence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Ohio State: consideration and initiating structure** (1950s) | two broad behaviours: caring for people, and organising the work | B: meta-analysis found consideration .48 and initiating structure .29 with leadership outcomes (Judge, Piccolo and Ilies 2004) |
| **Fiedler's contingency model** (1967) | a leader's fixed style suits some situations; change the situation, not the leader | C: mixed support; measurement criticised |
| **Path–goal theory** (House, 1971) | the leader clarifies the path to goals and removes obstacles; the best style depends on task and follower | C: partial support |
| **Situational leadership** (Hersey and Blanchard, 1969) | match directive and supportive behaviour to follower readiness | C: see [people management](/leadership/management/#situational-leadership) |
| **Leader–member exchange (LMX)** (Graen and colleagues) | leaders form higher-quality relationships with some followers than others; quality predicts outcomes | B: well studied, but largely correlational |

## Goleman's six leadership styles

Daniel Goleman, "Leadership That Gets Results" (*Harvard Business Review*, March–April 2000), drew on consultancy
Hay/McBer's data on 3,871 executives, selected from a database of more than 20,000. He found six styles, each linked to
components of emotional intelligence, and measured their effect on **climate**: flexibility, responsibility,
standards, rewards, clarity and commitment.

| Style | Modus operandi (paraphrased) | In a phrase | Effect on climate | Works best |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Coercive** | demands immediate compliance | "Do what I say" | **negative**; least effective in most situations | a genuine crisis, a turnaround, a problem employee after other options fail |
| **Authoritative** | mobilises people toward a vision; sets the end, leaves the means | "Come with me" | **most positive**; drives up every aspect of climate | when change needs a new vision or clear direction |
| **Affiliative** | creates harmony and emotional bonds | "People come first" | positive | healing rifts; motivating people through stressful times |
| **Democratic** | builds consensus through participation | asks for everyone's input | positive | building buy-in; when the leader is unsure and needs ideas |
| **Pacesetting** | sets very high standards and exemplifies them | do it as I do, now | **negative**; the article says it "destroys climate" | quick results from a highly motivated, highly competent team |
| **Coaching** | develops people for the future | try this | positive; also the least used | helping people who want to improve develop long-term strengths |

- The headline finding: leaders with the best results didn't rely on one style; they used most of them in a given
  week, depending on the situation. Goleman's analogy is a golfer choosing clubs.
- **Pacesetting is the founder-engineer's trap**: high personal standards, doing it yourself, impatience with slower
  people. Goleman expected it to help and found it corroded climate.
- The 2000 article organises emotional intelligence into four capabilities (self-awareness, self-management, social
  awareness, social skill), not the five components of his 1998 article (below).

**Evidence: C.** Proprietary consultancy data, not peer reviewed or independently replicated; climate was measured
by surveys of the same organisations. The style list is a good practical vocabulary; the precise effects are not
established science.

## Transformational vs transactional leadership

James MacGregor Burns (*Leadership*, 1978) contrasted **transactional** leadership (an exchange: work for reward)
with **transforming** leadership (leaders and followers raise each other's motivation and morality). Bernard Bass
(*Leadership and Performance Beyond Expectations*, 1985) turned it into a measurable theory; with Bruce Avolio he
built the **full-range leadership** model and the Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire (MLQ).

| Transformational: the four I's | Meaning | Looks like |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Idealised influence** | a role model people trust and want to emulate | lives the values; takes the hard option first |
| **Inspirational motivation** | articulates a compelling vision and high expectations | explains why the work matters; optimism about the goal |
| **Intellectual stimulation** | challenges assumptions; encourages new ideas | "What if we're wrong about X?"; no public ridicule of mistakes |
| **Individualised consideration** | attends to each follower's needs and growth | coaching; stretch assignments fitted to the person |

| Transactional and passive | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Contingent reward** | clear agreements: do X, get Y |
| **Management by exception, active** | watches for deviations and corrects them |
| **Management by exception, passive** | intervenes only when problems become serious |
| **Laissez-faire** | absent; avoids decisions |

| Meta-analytic result (Judge and Piccolo 2004, 87 sources) | Validity |
| :--- | :--- |
| transformational leadership | .44 |
| contingent reward | .39 |
| laissez-faire | −.37 |
| transformational with contingent reward | correlated .80 with each other |

Contingent reward sometimes predicted outcomes *better* than transformational leadership. Plain transactional
management (clear deals, kept promises) is underrated.

**The critique.** Daan van Knippenberg and Sim Sitkin (2013, *Academy of Management Annals*), "Back to the Drawing
Board?", identified four fundamental problems: no clear conceptual definition; no causal model for how each dimension
works; the construct is confounded with its effects (it's partly defined by success); and the standard questionnaires
don't reproduce the theory's dimensions. They recommended abandoning the charismatic–transformational label in
favour of more clearly defined behaviours.

**Evidence: B−.** Robust correlations with outcomes; serious doubts about what exactly is being measured.

## Servant leadership

Robert K. Greenleaf, "The Servant as Leader" (essay, 1970): the leader is servant first, and the best test is "Do
those served grow as persons? Do they become healthier, wiser, freer, more autonomous, more likely themselves to become
servants?" Larry Spears later listed ten characteristics: listening, empathy, healing, awareness, persuasion,
conceptualisation, foresight, stewardship, commitment to people's growth, and building community.

| In practice | Not |
| :--- | :--- |
| removing obstacles; asking "what do you need from me?" | doing whatever the team wants |
| developing people as a primary goal | avoiding hard decisions or standards |
| persuasion over positional authority | having no authority |

**Evidence: B−.** A meta-analysis (Hoch, Bommer, Dulebohn and Wu 2018, *Journal of Management*) found servant
leadership explained meaningful variance beyond transformational leadership, unlike authentic and ethical leadership,
which largely overlapped with it. Measurement is still inconsistent across studies. Critique: the "servant" framing
can drift into conflict avoidance and slow decisions.

## Level 5 leadership

Jim Collins, *Good to Great* (2001), studied 11 companies that moved from average to sustained outperformance and
found their CEOs combined **personal humility with intense professional will**.

| Level | Collins' label | Adds |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | highly capable individual | talent, knowledge, work habits |
| 2 | contributing team member | works well in a group |
| 3 | competent manager | organises people and resources |
| 4 | effective leader | catalyses commitment to a vision and high standards |
| 5 | Level 5 executive | humility plus will: ambition for the company, not themselves |

Collins' "window and mirror": credit goes out the window to others when things go well; the leader looks in the mirror
when things go badly.

**The critique.**

- **The companies declined.** Circuit City filed for bankruptcy in 2009; Fannie Mae was placed into government
  conservatorship in 2008; Wells Fargo later had its fake-accounts scandal. Steven Levitt noted that a portfolio of the
  11 bought when the book came out would have underperformed the S&P 500.
- **Halo effect.** Phil Rosenzweig (*The Halo Effect*, 2007) argued that the data came largely from press accounts and
  interviews written after success was known, so "humble, disciplined" leaders were partly attributions *caused by*
  good results.
- **Selecting on the outcome.** Picking winners and looking back for common traits can't separate causes from luck.
  Collins' own follow-up, *How the Mighty Fall* (2009), addresses the declines.

**Evidence: D** as a causal claim. The humility-plus-will description remains a useful personal ideal.

## Kouzes and Posner: five practices of exemplary leadership

James Kouzes and Barry Posner, *The Leadership Challenge* (first edition 1987), built from thousands of "personal best
leadership experience" case studies and their Leadership Practices Inventory (LPI).

| Practice | Meaning | Example behaviour |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Model the way** | clarify values; set the example | do what you say you will do |
| **Inspire a shared vision** | envision the future; enlist others | describe what success looks like in a year, in their terms |
| **Challenge the process** | seek opportunities; experiment and take risks | run small experiments; treat failures as learning |
| **Enable others to act** | foster collaboration; strengthen others | share information and decisions; build trust |
| **Encourage the heart** | recognise contributions; celebrate values and victories | specific, personal thanks; celebrate team wins |

**Evidence: C.** Much of the supporting research uses the authors' own instrument and self-reported "personal best"
stories. The practices overlap heavily with transformational leadership. A good, balanced checklist.

## Sinek's Golden Circle

Simon Sinek, *Start With Why* (2009) and the 2009 TED talk "How great leaders inspire action": inspiring leaders and
companies communicate from the inside out, **why → how → what**, rather than what → how → why.

- Useful as a communication rule: lead with purpose before features, especially when recruiting or pitching.
- Sinek claims the circle maps onto brain anatomy: the "what" to the neocortex (rational), the "why" and "how" to the
  limbic brain (feelings and decisions). This relies on the outdated "triune brain" model; neuroscientists don't
  support a neat rational neocortex vs emotional limbic split, and there's no evidence that purpose-first messages work
  *because* of this.
- The examples (Apple, the Wright brothers, Martin Luther King Jr.) are selected after the fact.

**Evidence: D** for the theory; the communication advice is reasonable common sense.

## Authentic leadership

Popularised by former Medtronic CEO Bill George (*Authentic Leadership*, 2003), and formalised by Fred Walumbwa and
colleagues (2008, *Journal of Management*) with four components:

| Component | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **self-awareness** | knowing your strengths, weaknesses and values |
| **relational transparency** | presenting your genuine self; sharing information openly |
| **balanced processing** | considering relevant data and opposing views before deciding |
| **internalised moral perspective** | behaviour guided by internal values, not group pressure |

**Evidence: C.** A meta-analysis (Banks, McCauley, Gardner and Guler 2016, *Leadership Quarterly*) found it
correlated about .72 with transformational leadership, raising doubts that it's a distinct construct; Hoch et al.
(2018) found little incremental value. Critique: "be yourself" can excuse bad behaviour ("I'm just being authentic");
effective leaders also adapt their style to situations.

## Adaptive leadership

Ronald Heifetz (*Leadership Without Easy Answers*, 1994; with Marty Linsky, *Leadership on the Line*, 2002; with
Alexander Grashow and Linsky, *The Practice of Adaptive Leadership*, 2009). Central distinction:

| | Technical problem | Adaptive challenge |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| definition | clear problem, known solution | problem unclear, or requires changes in people's values, habits or loyalties |
| who solves it | experts or authorities | the people with the problem |
| example | the database is slow: add an index | the team keeps shipping late because it avoids telling customers "no" |
| leader's mistake | — | treating an adaptive challenge as technical: new tool, new process, reorganisation |

Heifetz and Donald Laurie's "The Work of Leadership" (*Harvard Business Review*, 1997) lists principles, paraphrased:

- **Get on the balcony**: step out of the action (the "dance floor") to see patterns, including your own part in them.
- **Identify the adaptive challenge**: what values or habits have to change?
- **Regulate distress**: enough pressure to motivate change, not so much that people freeze or revolt.
- **Maintain disciplined attention**: keep people on the hard issue when they drift to avoidance.
- **Give the work back to the people**: don't solve it for them; they must own the change.
- **Protect voices of leadership from below**: don't silence the people raising uncomfortable questions.

**Evidence: C.** Conceptually strong and widely taught (Harvard Kennedy School), but based on cases and teaching
practice rather than systematic empirical testing.

## The trust equation

David Maister, Charles Green and Robert Galford, *The Trusted Advisor* (2000):

$$
T = \frac{C + R + I}{S}
$$

| Term | Meaning | Question it answers |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $C$ **credibility** | the words you speak: expertise, honesty | can I believe what they say? |
| $R$ **reliability** | your actions: doing what you said, consistently | do they do what they say? |
| $I$ **intimacy** | safety: people can share difficult things with you | is it safe to tell them things? |
| $S$ **self-orientation** | focus on yourself (ego, agenda, fear) vs on the other person | whose interests are they serving? |

Green's own view is that self-orientation, alone in the denominator, is the most powerful variable: high
self-orientation divides everything else away. Worked example: a technically brilliant lead (C = 9) who always
delivers (R = 8), whom nobody tells problems to (I = 3), and who visibly optimises for their own promotion (S = 8)
scores $20/8 = 2.5$. A slightly less expert peer with C = 7, R = 8, I = 7 and S = 3 scores $22/3 \approx 7.3$.

**Evidence: D.** A heuristic, not a measured model; the numbers are illustrative. Useful as a self-audit.

## Influence without authority and power bases

### Power bases (French and Raven)

John French and Bertram Raven (1959, in Cartwright, ed., *Studies in Social Power*) named five bases of social power;
Raven added a sixth, **informational**, in 1965.

| Base | Source | Durability | Founder-engineer example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **legitimate** | the formal role | lasts while the role does | "I'm the CTO" |
| **reward** | ability to give things people value | lasts while rewards do | promotions, equity, interesting projects |
| **coercive** | ability to punish | breeds compliance and resentment | firing, removing responsibilities |
| **expert** | knowledge and skill | durable, and domain-limited | you wrote the core system |
| **referent** | people identify with and admire you | durable; built slowly | people want to work with you |
| **informational** | the content of a persuasive argument or data | independent of the person | the dashboard that settles the debate |

Personal power (expert, referent, informational) tends to produce commitment; positional power (legitimate, reward,
coercive) tends to produce compliance. Coercive power used routinely destroys trust (see
[five dysfunctions](/leadership/five-dysfunctions/)).

### Influence without authority (Cohen and Bradford)

Allan Cohen and David Bradford (*Organizational Dynamics*, 1989; book *Influence Without Authority*, 1990) apply the
**law of reciprocity**: people help those who help them. Influence means trading "currencies" the other person values.

| Currency family | Examples |
| :--- | :--- |
| inspiration-related | a vision, excellence, doing the right thing |
| task-related | resources, help, cooperation, information |
| position-related | recognition, visibility, reputation, contacts |
| relationship-related | understanding, acceptance, personal support |
| personal-related | gratitude, ownership, a sense of self-worth |

The process: treat the other person as a potential ally; clarify your goals; diagnose their world (goals, pressures,
currencies); assess what you can offer; build the relationship; make the exchange. The mistake is assuming the other
side values what you value.

**Evidence: C** for both frameworks as practical tools. Power bases have been studied extensively, but measurement is
inconsistent.

## Emotional intelligence

Peter Salovey and John Mayer coined the academic concept in 1990; Daniel Goleman popularised it (*Emotional
Intelligence*, 1995) and applied it to leadership in "What Makes a Leader?" (*HBR*, 1998) with **five components**:
self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy and social skill.

| Model type | Measured by | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **ability EI** | performance tests with right answers | Mayer–Salovey–Caruso test (MSCEIT) |
| **self-report EI** | questionnaires about your own emotional skills | various |
| **mixed models** | questionnaires mixing emotional skills with personality and motivation | Goleman-style competency inventories |

**The debate.**

- Edwin Locke (2005, *Journal of Organizational Behavior*), "Why emotional intelligence is an invalid concept", argued
  the construct is so broad it becomes meaningless, and that it's really skills applied to emotion, not a separate
  intelligence.
- Mixed models overlap heavily with Big Five personality and cognitive ability, so critics ask what's new.
- Joseph and Newman (2010, *Journal of Applied Psychology*): ability EI predicted performance inconsistently:
  positively in jobs high in emotional labour, negatively in jobs low in it.
- O'Boyle et al. (2011, *Journal of Organizational Behavior*): the three streams of EI measures correlated .24–.30 with
  job performance, and self-report and mixed measures added prediction beyond IQ and the Big Five.
- Popular claims that EI matters more than IQ for success aren't supported by these meta-analyses.

**Evidence: C/B, contested.** The underlying skills (reading people, managing your own reactions) are clearly useful
for managers; treating EI as a single measurable intelligence is on weaker ground.

## The Peter principle

Laurence Peter and Raymond Hull, *The Peter Principle* (1969), a satirical book: in a hierarchy, people are promoted
until they reach their "level of incompetence", because promotion rewards performance in the current job rather than
aptitude for the next one.

- Benson, Li and Shue ("Promotions and the Peter Principle", *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2019), using data on
  sales workers at 214 firms, found evidence consistent with it: firms promoted the best salespeople, who then
  performed worse as managers than colleagues with better predictors of managerial ability.
- The founder-engineer version: promoting the best engineer to engineering manager by default.

Countermeasures: separate individual-contributor and management career tracks with equal status and pay; judge
promotion to management on management signals (mentoring, coordination, judgement), not output; make it reversible
without stigma.

**Evidence: B** that it happens in at least some settings; how general it is remains unclear.

## High standards (Bezos)

Jeff Bezos' 2017 letter to shareholders on high standards, paraphrased except where quoted:

| Claim | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| "high standards are teachable" | people learn them through exposure; a team with high standards spreads them to new members |
| "high standards are domain specific" | you can have exacting standards for code and poor ones for writing; each must be learned separately |
| you have to recognise what good looks like | you can't hit a standard you can't see; show examples |
| you need realistic expectations of scope | underestimating how hard something is produces bad work; his example is that a handstand takes about six months of daily practice, not two weeks |

His application is the six-page memo (see [people management](/leadership/management/#meetings)): memos often fail not
because the writer can't recognise a great memo, but because they expected it to take a day or two when it takes a week
or more.

**Evidence: D** (one CEO's practice), but consistent with research on deliberate practice and clear performance
standards. For a small team: write down what "great" looks like for the few things that matter most (a PR, a design
doc, an incident report), with examples, and say how long it should take.

## Followership

Leadership only exists in relation to followers, and most people spend most of their careers following.

| Model | Core idea |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Robert Kelley**, "In Praise of Followers" (*HBR*, 1988; book *The Power of Followership*, 1992) | two dimensions: independent critical thinking, and active engagement. Five types, with labels that vary between versions: exemplary (star) followers are high on both; alienated followers think critically but disengage; conformists (yes-people) engage without thinking critically; passive followers (sheep) do neither; pragmatists sit in the middle |
| **Ira Chaleff**, *The Courageous Follower* (1995) | followers should assume responsibility, serve, challenge, participate in transformation, and take moral action, including refusing when necessary |
| **Barbara Kellerman**, *Followership* (2008) | classifies followers by engagement: isolates, bystanders, participants, activists, diehards |

For a leader: build a team of exemplary followers by rewarding challenge (see psychological safety in
[five dysfunctions](/leadership/five-dysfunctions/)), and be a good follower to your own board, investors or boss.

**Evidence: C.** Conceptual models with limited empirical testing; Kelley's typology is widely used in teaching.

## Which model when

| Situation | Reach for | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| a crisis, outage or genuine emergency | Goleman's coercive or authoritative style, briefly; [OODA](/leadership/ooda-loop/) | speed matters; explain afterwards |
| a new team with no shared direction | authoritative / transformational: inspirational motivation | people need a why and a destination |
| an experienced team executing a clear plan | transactional contingent reward plus [mission command](/leadership/mission-command/) | clear deals, then get out of the way |
| someone new to a task | situational leadership S1/S2; high structure | low task-relevant maturity needs direction |
| a strong performer who wants to grow | coaching style; GROW; individualised consideration | develop judgement, not dependence |
| the team avoids a hard truth (culture, customers, strategy) | adaptive leadership: balcony, regulate distress, give the work back | a technical fix won't work |
| you need something from a team you don't manage | influence without authority; informational and referent power | you have no positional power there |
| trust is low | trust equation self-audit; [five dysfunctions](/leadership/five-dysfunctions/) | find the weak term, usually intimacy or self-orientation |
| you're tempted to do it all yourself | beware pacesetting; Grove's managerial leverage | your output is the team's output |
| promoting someone to manager | Peter principle safeguards | management is a different job |

## Self-assessment checklist

Score each 1 (rarely) to 5 (consistently). Anything at 2 or below is your development goal for the quarter. Ask two
team members to score you too; the gaps are more informative than the scores.

```text
DIRECTION
[ ] My team can state our goal and why it matters in one
    sentence (inspirational motivation, compelling direction)
[ ] I explain the reasons behind decisions, not just the
    decisions
[ ] I make clear deals: what's expected, what they get,
    and I keep my side (contingent reward)

PEOPLE
[ ] I adjust how closely I supervise to each person's
    experience with the specific task (TRM / situational)
[ ] I spend time on each person's growth, not only their
    output (individualised consideration, servant)
[ ] I give specific, timely feedback, positive and
    corrective, in private

SELF
[ ] I admit mistakes to the team, promptly
[ ] I notice when I'm pacesetting or coercing, and why
[ ] My self-orientation is low: people believe I'm
    working for them and the company, not my career
[ ] I know my own blind spots and have asked for input
    on them in the last 3 months

CHANGE AND INFLUENCE
[ ] I can tell a technical problem from an adaptive one,
    and I don't reorganise to avoid a hard conversation
[ ] I get things done with peers and other teams without
    escalating (influence without authority)
[ ] I reward people who challenge me with good arguments
    (followership, psychological safety)

STANDARDS
[ ] I've shown the team examples of what "great" looks
    like for our key outputs
[ ] My expectations of how long good work takes are
    realistic
```
