How to turn a goal into a plan: principles, FITT-VP, periodisation, load monitoring, deloads, tapers, combining
lifting with running, and what to do when life gets in the way. Lifting variables are in
[strength training](/fitness/training/strength-training/), running zones and sessions in
[endurance](/fitness/training/endurance/), and recovery habits in
[stretching and recovery](/fitness/recovery-mobility/stretching-recovery/).

## Training principles

| Principle | Meaning | In practice |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| specificity (SAID) | Specific Adaptation to Imposed Demands: you adapt to what you do | train the energy system, speed, load and movement of the goal; long runs for a marathon, heavy singles for a 1RM |
| progressive overload | the stimulus must rise as you adapt | add load, reps, sets, distance or pace, one variable at a time |
| individualisation | people respond differently to the same plan | set loads from your own tests and logs, not someone else's plan numbers |
| reversibility | adaptations fade when training stops | see [detraining](#detraining-and-maintenance); keep a minimal dose |
| variation | change the stimulus to keep progressing and limit staleness | rotate rep ranges, exercises or session types every few weeks; keep the core lifts and runs |
| recovery | adaptation happens between sessions | plan easy days and deload weeks; sleep and food count |
| supercompensation | load, fatigue, then recovery to above the starting level | a simplified model (after Selye); real responses are messier and depend on non-training stress too (Kiely 2018) |
| overreaching | short planned overload with a temporary dip in performance | *functional* overreaching recovers in days to weeks; *non-functional* takes weeks to months; overtraining syndrome takes months (Meeusen et al. 2013) |

## FITT-VP

ACSM's frame for writing an exercise prescription. Change one letter at a time when you progress.

| Letter | Means | Running example | Lifting example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| F | frequency | 4 runs/week | 3 sessions/week, each muscle 2× |
| I | intensity | 80% easy, 20% hard by time | 70–85% 1RM, RPE 7–9 |
| T | time | 30–100 min per run | 45–75 min per session |
| T | type | road, trail, treadmill | barbell, dumbbells, machines |
| V | volume | 35 km/week | 12 hard sets per muscle per week |
| P | progression | long run +1 km per loading week | +2.5 kg or +1 rep per week |

The ACSM 2011 baseline for healthy adults is 150 min/week of moderate cardio (or 75 min vigorous), resistance
training 2–3 days/week, and balance work 2–3 days/week. WHO 2020 sets 150–300 min moderate or 75–150 min
vigorous, plus muscle strengthening on at least 2 days.

## Needs analysis and goal setting

1. **Goal**: one primary, specific and dated ("half marathon under 1:50 on 15 March"; "squat 1.5 × body mass").
   At most 1–2 secondary goals.
2. **Demands**: which energy systems, strength qualities and skills the goal needs
   ([energy systems](/fitness/training/energy-systems/)); race distance, terrain, heat.
3. **Baseline**: training age, current weekly volume, recent tests (5 km time trial, estimated 1RM, resting HR),
   injury history.
4. **Gap**: demands minus baseline. Rank what limits you most.
5. **Constraints**: days per week, minutes per session, equipment, travel, work and family peaks, sleep.
6. **Plan backwards** from the event date: race, taper, peak, build, base (see the diagram below).
7. **Checkpoints**: a test or benchmark session every 4–8 weeks; adjust the plan, not just the goal.

| Goal check | Ask |
| :--- | :--- |
| specific | what exact distance, lift, time or weight? |
| measurable | how will you test it? |
| achievable | is it a realistic step from your recent results, current volume and training age? |
| relevant | does it serve what you care about this year? |
| time-bound | what is the date? |

## Periodisation

Periodisation splits the time before a goal into phases, each with its own emphasis.

| Level | Length | Purpose | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| macrocycle | months to a year | the whole build to a goal or season | 16–24 weeks to a marathon |
| mesocycle | usually 3–6 weeks | one emphasis, often ending in a deload | 3 loading weeks + 1 deload |
| microcycle | usually 1 week | how sessions and rest days are arranged | Tue intervals, Sat long run |
| session | one workout | the unit you log | 6 × 3 min at 5 km pace |

### Models

| Model | Pattern | Suited to |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| traditional (linear) | volume high then falling, intensity low then rising, over months | beginners; one main event |
| block | 2–6 weeks accumulation (volume, general work), 2–4 weeks transmutation (specific, harder work), 8–15 days realisation (taper and peak) (Issurin 2010) | trained athletes, several peaks a season |
| undulating (DUP, weekly) | load and reps change day to day or week to week | intermediate lifters; mixed goals |
| reverse linear | intensity first, then volume | ultra and long events, where specific volume comes last |
| polarised / pyramidal intensity | most running easy, a small share hard | endurance: see [endurance](/fitness/training/endurance/) |

### What the evidence says

| Finding | Source |
| :--- | :--- |
| periodised plans beat non-periodised plans for 1RM (effect size 0.43) | Williams et al. 2017, 18 studies |
| with equal volume, periodised beat non-periodised for 1RM (ES 0.31) but not for hypertrophy; undulating beat linear for 1RM only in trained lifters | Moesgaard et al. 2022, 35 studies |
| linear and daily undulating give similar hypertrophy | Grgic et al. 2017 |
| across 137 reviews, periodisation did not consistently change outcomes for average healthy adults | ACSM 2026 |
| the physiological basis of classic periodisation is weaker than its popularity suggests | Kiely 2018 |

Reading: structure helps you progress and schedule recovery, but the specific model matters less than consistency,
enough volume and managing fatigue.

## Load monitoring

**Session RPE** (Foster et al. 2001): about 30 min after the session, rate the whole session on the CR-10 scale.

$$
\text{session load (AU)} = \text{session RPE} \times \text{duration (min)}
$$

| CR-10 | Session felt |
| :--- | :--- |
| 0 | rest |
| 1 | very, very easy |
| 2 | easy |
| 3 | moderate |
| 4 | somewhat hard |
| 5 | hard |
| 6 | (between 5 and 7) |
| 7 | very hard |
| 8–9 | (between 7 and 10) |
| 10 | maximal |

| Metric | Formula | Use |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| weekly load | sum of session loads | the trend week to week |
| monotony | mean daily load ÷ SD of daily load (rest days = 0) | high means every day is the same; add easy and rest days (Foster 1998) |
| strain | weekly load × monotony | in Foster 1998, most illnesses followed weeks above the athlete's own strain threshold |
| ACWR | 7-day load ÷ 28-day average weekly load | contested, see below |

### How fast to increase

| Claim | Evidence |
| :--- | :--- |
| "never increase weekly mileage by more than 10%" | **folklore.** In a trial of 532 novice runners, a 13-week plan built on the 10% rule gave the same injury rate as a standard 8-week plan (20.8% vs 20.3%) (Buist et al. 2008) |
| big weekly jumps are risky | novices who raised weekly distance by more than 30% over 2 weeks had more distance-related injuries than those under 10% (HR 1.59, 95% CI 0.96–2.66; exploratory) (Nielsen et al. 2014) |
| single-run spikes matter more than weekly change | in 5 205 runners, a run more than 10% longer than the longest run of the previous 30 days raised the overuse-injury rate: HRR 1.64 for 10–30% longer, 1.52 for 30–100%, 2.28 for over 100%. The week-to-week ratio showed no relationship (Frandsen et al. 2025) |
| keep ACWR at 0.8–1.3, danger at ≥ 1.5 | proposed from team-sport data (Gabbett 2016). Later critiques found no causal evidence, plus statistical artefacts from the ratio, and advise against using it to manage injury risk (Impellizzeri et al. 2020) |

Practical rule: there is no proven weekly limit. Grow volume steadily (many coaches use 5–10% in loading weeks) with a step back every 3–4 weeks. Keep
each long run within about 10% of the longest run of the past month. Watch sleep, soreness and mood as well as
the numbers.

## Deloads and recovery weeks

A deload is a planned period of reduced training stress to shed fatigue and prepare for the next block (Bell et al.
2023).

| Question | Evidence or consensus |
| :--- | :--- |
| how often? | every 4–6 weeks, by coach consensus (Bell et al. 2023); strength athletes reported every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks (Rogerson et al. 2024) |
| how long? | about 7 days (reported 6.4 ± 1.7 days); anything from one session to 2 weeks |
| what changes? | fewer sets and reps, lighter loads, more reps in reserve; frequency and exercise choice mostly unchanged. Complete rest is not the default |
| how much less? | not trial-tested. Common practice cuts sets by 30–50%, drops load about 10% and adds 2–3 RIR. For comparison, strength tapers of 30–50% less volume worked better than 50–70% (Travis et al. 2020) |
| is full rest as good? | one week off in the middle of a 9-week block gave similar muscle growth but slightly smaller strength gains than training straight through (Coleman et al. 2024) |
| runners | a recovery week at about 60–80% of recent volume, with strides or short pickups to keep speed (coaching practice) |
| reactive deload | take one early if performance drops for 2+ sessions, joints ache, sleep is poor or motivation sinks |

## Tapering

A taper is a progressive, non-linear cut in training load before a goal event, to shed fatigue while keeping
fitness.

| Variable | Guidance | Source |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| duration | 2 weeks was optimal in the meta-analysis; 4 to over 28 days in the literature | Bosquet et al. 2007; Mujika & Padilla 2003 |
| volume | cut by 41–60%, exponentially (bigger cuts near the event) | Bosquet et al. 2007 |
| intensity | **keep it**: short efforts at race pace or faster | Bosquet et al. 2007; Mujika & Padilla 2003 |
| frequency | keep it; cut by no more than about 20% | Bosquet et al. 2007; Mujika & Padilla 2003 |
| expected gain | about 3% (usual range 0.5–6%) | Mujika & Padilla 2003 |
| marathon (recreational) | strict, steadily falling 3-week tapers were a median 5 min 32 s (2.6%) faster than a minimal taper, in 158 000 Strava runners | Smyth & Lawlor 2021 |
| max strength | 1–2 weeks, volume −30–70% (−30–50% best), load ≥ 85% 1RM, then 2–7 days of little or no lifting | Travis et al. 2020 |

## Concurrent strength and endurance

| Issue | Guidance | Source |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| interference | max strength and muscle growth are not blunted by adding cardio; explosive strength is | Schumann et al. 2022 |
| same session vs apart | explosive strength suffered more in the same session than with ≥ 3 h between | Schumann et al. 2022 |
| hours between | 0 h (back to back) gave the least strength gain, 6 h was better, 24 h best | Robineau et al. 2016 |
| cardio mode | running interfered with lifting more than cycling; longer and more frequent endurance work interfered more | Wilson et al. 2012 |

| Priority | Order and spacing |
| :--- | :--- |
| strength first | lift first, or run in the morning and lift over 6 h later; easy cardio only after lifting |
| endurance first | run key sessions fresh; lift after the run the same day (≥ 6 h if possible) or on easy days |
| equal | put hard runs and heavy lifts on the same days to keep easy days easy; no heavy legs the day before a key run |
| same session unavoidable | put the priority first; keep cardio short and easy, or cycle rather than run |

## Adjusting for life

| Situation | What to do |
| :--- | :--- |
| missed 1 session | skip it and carry on; don't stack two hard days together |
| missed a week | repeat the previous week, then continue |
| short on time | keep intensity, cut volume: fewer sets, shorter runs with the key segment kept (Spiering et al. 2021) |
| travel | bodyweight, bands or hotel-gym sessions; easy runs to explore; count long travel days as hard days |
| work or family stress, poor sleep | non-training stress adds to fatigue; swap a hard day for an easy one and move the key session |
| cold above the neck only (runny nose, sore throat, sneezing) | may train easy by the "neck check" rule; stop if you feel worse (Metz 2003) |
| below the neck (fever, chest cold, cough, body aches, stomach bug) | don't train. Resume easy after symptoms and fever have gone |
| after illness, see a doctor | chest pain, palpitations, fainting, or breathlessness out of proportion to effort |
| niggle or pain | pain that changes your gait or technique, or gets worse during a session: stop and cross-train; persistent pain: see a physio or doctor |

## Detraining and maintenance

| Stopped completely | What happens | Source |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 10 days | metabolic shifts: more carbohydrate use, lower insulin sensitivity | Mujika & Padilla 2001 |
| 12–21 days | VO₂max down about 7% in trained endurance athletes; muscle oxidative enzymes fall with a half-time of about 12 days | Coyle et al. 1984 |
| over 4 weeks | recently gained VO₂max is lost completely; long-trained athletes stay above untrained levels | Mujika & Padilla 2001 |
| 56–84 days | VO₂max stabilises about 16% below trained, still above sedentary controls | Coyle et al. 1984 |
| strength | declines more slowly than endurance and stays above baseline for long periods; bigger losses with longer breaks, in people over 65 and in untrained people | Mujika & Padilla 2000; Bosquet et al. 2013 |

**Minimal dose to maintain** (Spiering et al. 2021, general populations):

| Quality | Keep | Can cut |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| endurance | intensity (heart rate) | frequency to 2 sessions/week, or volume by 33–66% (13–26 min per session), for up to 15 weeks |
| strength and size, younger adults | relative load | to 1 session/week and 1 set per exercise, for up to 32 weeks |
| muscle size, older adults | relative load | to about 2 sessions/week and 2–3 sets per exercise |

## Tracking and logs

Log each session as you go and review the log once a week. The log is what makes individualisation and
autoregulation possible.

```text
Date   Session         Min  sRPE  Load  Details                         Sleep  Sore  Notes
06-02  Easy run        45   3     135   8.1 km @ 5:33/km, HR 138        7.5 h  2/5
06-03  Intervals       60   7     420   6x3 min @ 4:25/km, 2 min jog    7 h    2/5   legs heavy on rep 5
06-04  Lower (heavy)   60   6     360   Squat 4x5 @ 100 kg RPE 8        7 h    3/5
                                        RDL 3x6 @ 80 kg RPE 7
Week   Total km 42  Runs 4  Lifts 2  Load 1785 AU  Monotony 1.4  Longest run 16 km
```

| Track | Why |
| :--- | :--- |
| sets × reps × kg @ RPE | progression decisions, 1RM estimates |
| distance, time, pace, HR | fitness trends; long-run spikes |
| session RPE × minutes | one load number for running and lifting |
| sleep, soreness (1–5), mood | early warning before performance drops |
| body mass (weekly average), illness, niggles | fuelling and injury patterns |
| tests (5 km TT, estimated 1RM) | checkpoints every 4–8 weeks |

## Recipes

### 12-week half-marathon outline
For a runner already doing about 25 km/week over 4 runs with a 10 km long run. There are two 3:1 blocks, two more build
weeks, then a 2-week taper. The long run never grows more than 10% past the longest of the last 30 days.

| Week | km | Long run | Key session |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | 26 | 10 km | 6 × 20 s strides |
| 2 | 28 | 11 km | 6 × 1 min hills |
| 3 | 30 | 12 km | 3 × 6 min at threshold |
| 4 (deload) | 23 | 9 km | strides only |
| 5 | 31 | 13 km | 3 × 8 min at threshold |
| 6 | 33 | 14 km | 5 × 1 km at 10 km pace |
| 7 | 35 | 15 km | 2 × 12 min at threshold |
| 8 (deload) | 27 | 11 km | strides only |
| 9 | 36 | 16 km, last 4 at half-marathon pace | 4 × 2 km at half-marathon pace |
| 10 | 38 | 17 km, last 5 at half-marathon pace | 3 × 10 min at threshold |
| 11 (taper) | 27 | 12 km | 3 × 2 km at half-marathon pace |
| 12 (race) | 15 + race | race | 2 × 1 km at race pace early in the week |

Keep strength work at 2 sessions a week (see [strength training](/fitness/training/strength-training/)), and halve it
in weeks 11–12. Paces and zones are in [endurance](/fitness/training/endurance/).

### 8-week strength block with deloads
For one main lift (such as the squat), twice a week with 2–3 assistance lifts. The first three weeks build
volume, the next three build intensity, and each ends in a deload.

| Week | Phase | Main lift | Assistance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | accumulation | 4 × 8 at RPE 7 | 3 × 10–12 |
| 2 | accumulation | 4 × 8 at RPE 7.5 | 3 × 10–12 |
| 3 | accumulation | 5 × 8 at RPE 8 | 4 × 10–12 |
| 4 | deload | 2 × 8 at about 90% of week 3's load, RPE 6 | 2 × 10 |
| 5 | intensification | 4 × 5 at RPE 7.5 | 3 × 6–8 |
| 6 | intensification | 5 × 4 at RPE 8 | 3 × 6–8 |
| 7 | intensification | 5 × 3 at RPE 8.5 | 3 × 6–8 |
| 8 | deload + test | 2 × 3 at RPE 6 early in the week; test a single at RPE 9 or a 3RM at the end | 1–2 × 8 |

### Hybrid week: lift and run
For balanced goals: 4 runs and 3 lifts. Hard running and heavy legs share a day, at least 6 h apart, so the easy
days stay easy.

```text
Day  AM                                   PM
Mon  Easy run 40 min                      Upper body 45 min
Tue  Intervals 6 x 3 min @ 5 km pace      Lower heavy: squat 4x5, RDL 3x6 (6+ h later)
Wed  Rest or 30 min easy spin / mobility  -
Thu  Tempo 25 min @ threshold             Upper body + core 40 min
Fri  Rest                                 -
Sat  Long run 80-100 min easy             -
Sun  Easy run 30 min + 6 strides          Lower light: split squat, calf raise, hops 30 min
```

### Marathon taper, 3 weeks
Starting from a peak week of 80 km. Volume falls steadily, intensity stays, and the number of runs stays the
same.

| Week | Volume | Long run | Keep |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| race −3 | 64 km (−20%) | 26 km with 10 km at marathon pace | one threshold session |
| race −2 | 48 km (−40%) | 18 km with 8 km at marathon pace | 5 × 1 km at threshold |
| race week | 30 km before the race (−60%) | none | Tue: 3 × 2 km at marathon pace; 2 short runs with strides |

- Last heavy lift about 10 days out. After that, bodyweight or light work only; no new exercises.
- Carbohydrate loading and race fuelling are in [endurance](/fitness/training/endurance/) and
  [nutrition and hydration](/fitness/nutrition/nutrition-hydration/).

### Session-RPE load, worked
One week, logged with the CR-10 scale about 30 min after each session.

```text
Day  Session        min  sRPE  Load (AU)
Mon  Easy run       45   3     135
Tue  Intervals      60   7     420
Wed  Lift           60   6     360
Thu  Easy run       40   3     120
Fri  Rest            0   0       0
Sat  Long run      100   5     500
Sun  Lift           50   5     250

Weekly load  = 1785 AU
Mean daily   = 1785 / 7 = 255 AU
SD (sample)  = 181 AU
Monotony     = 255 / 181 = 1.41
Strain       = 1785 x 1.41 = 2520
Last week 1600 AU -> +12%: fine in a loading week if
sleep and soreness are normal; next week is a deload.
```

### Return after a break
A rule of thumb, not trial-tested. Scale it to how long you were off and why (illness needs a slower start).
Rebuild endurance before intensity.

| Time off | Week 1 | Week 2 | Week 3 | Week 4 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| up to 1 week | about 90% volume, all easy | normal | – | – |
| 2–3 weeks | 60–70% volume, easy only | 80%, add strides | 90%, one quality session | normal |
| 4–8 weeks | 50%, easy, run-walk if needed | 60–65% | 75% + strides | 85%, one quality session, then build as a new base |
| lifting (any break over 2 weeks) | loads −10–20%, 1 set fewer, 3–4 RIR | loads −5–10%, 2 RIR | back to previous loads | progress normally |

- Long runs: follow the 10% single-run rule against the longest run of the last 30 days, not your pre-break
  long run (Frandsen et al. 2025).
- After a fever or chest infection, start at the bottom row, whatever the time off.
