The practical craft of managing a small team: what a manager's output is, one-on-ones, delegation, feedback,
coaching, goals, performance problems, hiring, meetings, decision rights, managing up and workload, with templates
and scripts for the hard conversations. Team-level dysfunction is in [five dysfunctions](/leadership/five-dysfunctions/),
intent-based delegation in [mission command](/leadership/mission-command/), owning outcomes in
[extreme ownership](/leadership/extreme-ownership/), the theories behind all of this in
[leadership models](/leadership/leadership-models/), and decision frameworks in
[decision-making models](/thinking/mental-models/decision-making/).

## The manager's job

Andy Grove's *High Output Management* (1983) is still the best single definition. Paraphrased:

$$
\text{manager's output} = \text{output of their organization} + \text{output of neighboring organizations they influence}
$$

Your own tickets, code and deals count only as far as they raise the team's output. A founder-engineer who ships the
most code while the team stalls has low managerial output.

### Managerial leverage

Grove treats every managerial activity as having a **leverage**: the output it produces per hour spent. Total output
is the sum of activities times their leverage, so the job is to pick high-leverage activities.

| High leverage when… | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| one action affects many people | setting the quarter's priorities; writing an engineering principles doc |
| a brief intervention changes someone's work for a long time | a one-hour one-on-one that unblocks a month of work; training |
| you supply a unique piece of knowledge or information | the customer context only you have; the reason behind a decision |

| Negative leverage (Grove's examples and their modern forms) | Why it hurts |
| :--- | :--- |
| **meddling**: doing a report's work for them, rewriting their PR | removes their ownership and learning; you become the bottleneck |
| **indecision** or late decisions | the whole team waits |
| canceling one-on-ones "because we're busy" | problems surface late, when they're expensive |
| being unavailable, then swooping in | teaches people to wait for you |

### Task-relevant maturity

Grove argues the right management style depends not on the person in general but on their **task-relevant maturity
(TRM)**: their experience and skill with *this particular task*. The idea closely parallels situational
leadership (below). A senior engineer starting in a new domain has low TRM there.

| TRM | Effective style (Grove, paraphrased) | In practice |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| low | structured, task-oriented: what, when and how | clear specs, frequent check-ins, worked examples |
| medium | individual-oriented: two-way communication, support, mutual reasoning | discuss approaches, let them choose, review together |
| high | minimal involvement: agree objectives, then monitor | outcome goals, regular but light reporting |

The failure modes: micromanaging a high-TRM person (they leave) and abandoning a low-TRM person with "you've got
this" (they flounder quietly). **Monitoring is not the same as meddling**: even high-TRM work needs a check-in cadence.

## One-on-ones

A recurring private meeting between a manager and each direct report. Grove's view: it's the **report's meeting**, for
their agenda, and it should be long enough to reach real issues. His point about leverage is that a short, regular
investment of your time can raise the quality of their work for weeks.

| Purpose | Not the purpose |
| :--- | :--- |
| surface problems early, while they're cheap | a status update you could read in a tracker |
| coaching, feedback, career growth | the manager's to-do list |
| trust and context in both directions | performance review ambush |
| hear what they won't say in a group | canceled whenever something else comes up |

| Cadence | When | Length |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| weekly | the default; new reports; low TRM; a lot is changing | 30–45 min |
| fortnightly | experienced, high-TRM reports in a stable period | 45–60 min |
| monthly | skip-levels (your reports' reports); mentoring | 30–45 min |
| ad hoc | never a substitute for the recurring slot | as needed |

```text
ONE-ON-ONE AGENDA (shared doc, newest at top)
Date: ____

Their items (first, and most of the time)
- ...

My items
- context they need: decisions, changes, what's coming
- feedback (one piece, specific)

Standing questions (rotate; don't ask all every week)
- What's taking more energy than it should?
- Anything I should know before I hear it elsewhere?

Career / growth (monthly)
- Progress on the growth goal we agreed

Actions
- [ ] Owner - action - by when
```

### Question bank

| Topic | Questions |
| :--- | :--- |
| opening | What's on your mind this week? What should we make sure we cover? |
| blockers | What's slowing you down? If you could remove one obstacle, which? What are you waiting on? |
| workload | On a scale of 1–10, how sustainable is your load? What would you drop if you had to? |
| team | Who's been great to work with lately? Is there tension I should know about? What's one thing we should stop doing as a team? |
| manager feedback | What's one thing I could do differently to help you more? What am I doing that I should stop? |
| clarity | Are priorities clear? Is there anything you're doing that you're unsure matters? |
| growth | What do you want to be doing in 1–2 years? What skill would make the biggest difference? What work would stretch you? |
| engagement | What part of your work do you look forward to? What part do you dread? What would make you think about leaving? |
| closing | What did we decide? What do you need from me by when? |

## Delegation

Delegation is not binary. Management 3.0 (Jurgen Appelo, *Management 3.0*, 2011) defines **seven levels of
delegation**, used in the "delegation poker" exercise and a **delegation board** (a grid of decision areas × levels,
visible to the team).

| Level | Name | Who decides |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | **Tell** | I decide and tell you |
| 2 | **Sell** | I decide and try to convince you |
| 3 | **Consult** | I ask for your input, then decide |
| 4 | **Agree** | we decide together |
| 5 | **Advise** | I give advice; you decide |
| 6 | **Inquire** | you decide; I ask about it afterward |
| 7 | **Delegate** | you decide; I don't need to know |

```text
DELEGATION BOARD (example: 5-person product team)
Decision area              | Level | Notes
Choice of library          |   7   | follow the tech radar
Sprint scope               |   4   | agree in planning
Hiring a new engineer      |   3   | team interviews, I decide
Production database schema |   5   | ask the staff engineer
Pricing changes            |   2   | founder decides, explains
Customer refunds under 500 |   6   | support lead, weekly log
```

How to hand over a task well:

- Define the **outcome and why it matters**, the constraints, and the deadline, not the method (see
  [mission command](/leadership/mission-command/) for commander's intent).
- Name the delegation level explicitly: "You decide; tell me afterward."
- Agree the check-in points and what "done" means before work starts.
- Delegate whole problems where possible, including the interesting parts, not just the leftovers.
- When it's done differently from how you'd have done it, ask whether it's *worse* or just *different*.

### Situational leadership

Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard proposed the "life cycle theory of leadership" in 1969, renamed **Situational
Leadership** in the 1970s. Style should match the follower's readiness for the specific task. Blanchard later
produced his own version, **SLII** (1985, in *Leadership and the One Minute Manager*), with different labels, and the
two versions are now sold by separate companies.

| Style | Original (Hersey–Blanchard) | SLII (Blanchard) | Directive / supportive | Matches |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| S1 | **Telling** | **Directing** | high / low | R1: unable and unwilling or insecure · D1: enthusiastic beginner (low competence, high commitment) |
| S2 | **Selling** | **Coaching** | high / high | R2: unable but willing or confident · D2: disillusioned learner (some competence, low commitment) |
| S3 | **Participating** | **Supporting** | low / high | R3: able but unwilling or insecure · D3: capable but cautious performer |
| S4 | **Delegating** | **Delegating** | low / low | R4: able and willing · D4: self-reliant achiever |

Note the two versions order readiness differently: in SLII the beginner starts *enthusiastic* (D1) and dips in
commitment at D2. Evidence quality: widely taught, weakly supported. Empirical tests, for example Thompson and Vecchio
(2009, *Leadership Quarterly*), found little support for its specific prescriptions. Use it as a prompt ("am I
over- or under-supervising this person on this task?"), not as a science.

## Feedback

### SBI and SBII

The Center for Creative Leadership's **Situation–Behavior–Impact** model keeps feedback specific and about
behavior, not character. The SBII variant adds **Intent**: ask what they were trying to do.

| Step | Do | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Situation** | when and where, specifically | "In this morning's planning meeting…" |
| **Behavior** | observable action, no judgment | "…you cut Priya off twice while she was presenting the estimate…" |
| **Impact** | the effect on you, the team, the work | "…she stopped talking and we committed to a date she thinks is wrong." |
| **Intent** (SBII) | ask, then listen | "What was going on for you?" |

Bad: "You're rude in meetings." Good: the table above. For praise, the same structure makes it believable and
repeatable: "In yesterday's incident, you posted updates every 15 minutes; support could answer customers without
pinging us."

### Radical Candor

Kim Scott's *Radical Candor* (2017) crosses two axes: **care personally** and **challenge directly**.

| | Low challenge | High challenge |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **High care** | **ruinous empathy**: nice, vague, lets problems grow | **radical candor**: kind and clear |
| **Low care** | **manipulative insincerity**: flattery to your face, criticism behind your back | **obnoxious aggression**: "brutal honesty" without care |

- Most new managers fail into **ruinous empathy**: they like their reports and avoid the discomfort, so the person
  finds out about the problem at review time or when they're let go.
- Scott's framing is about behavior in a given moment, not a personality label ("you did something obnoxiously
  aggressive", not "you are an obnoxious aggressor").
- Ask for criticism before you give it, and show you can take it.

### Why not the feedback sandwich

Praise, criticism, praise. It's discouraged because:

- the criticism gets lost or softened, so the person remembers the praise and misses the point;
- people learn the pattern and start discounting praise as a warm-up for bad news;
- it feels manipulative to experienced recipients, which damages trust.

Instead: give praise and criticism separately and often, each specific. The evidence base for any particular feedback
format is thin; what's consistently supported is that feedback should be timely, specific, about behavior and the
task rather than the person.

| Feedback rule | Why |
| :--- | :--- |
| soon: within a day or two | memory is fresh; it's clearly about this event |
| private for criticism, public or private for praise | public criticism triggers defense, not learning |
| one issue at a time | a list feels like an attack and none of it lands |
| about behavior, not traits | behavior can change; "you're careless" can't be acted on |
| ask, then listen | there's often context you don't have |
| follow up | notice and say when the behavior changes |

## Coaching

**GROW** was first published by John Whitmore in *Coaching for Performance* (1992); the name is credited to Max
Landsberg and Graham Alexander, and the model was in use before it was named. Coaching means helping someone find
their own answer; use it when they have the ability and need to build judgment. When someone lacks the knowledge,
teach instead.

| Stage | Aim | Questions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Goal** | what they want from this conversation and beyond | What would a great outcome look like? What do you want to leave this conversation with? |
| **Reality** | the current situation, facts not opinions | What's happening now? What have you tried? What's in your control? What's the evidence? |
| **Options** | possible actions, widely | What could you do? What else? What would you do if you had no constraints? What would someone you admire do? |
| **Will / way forward** | commitment | What will you do, by when? What might get in the way? How committed are you, 1–10? What would make it a 10? |

Coaching habits that matter more than the model:

- Ask one question at a time, then stay quiet.
- Resist giving the answer; if you must, offer it as one option among several.
- "What else?" is the most useful follow-up.
- End with a specific commitment and a date.

## Goals: OKRs and SMART

### OKRs

**Objectives and key results** came from Andy Grove at Intel in the 1970s (described in *High Output Management*).
John Doerr learned them at Intel in 1975 and introduced them to Google in 1999; *Measure What Matters* (2018) is his
book on them.

- **Objective**: qualitative, inspiring, time-bound: *what* you want.
- **Key results**: 3–5 measurable outcomes that show the objective was met: *how you'll know*.
- Google distinguishes **committed** OKRs (expected to be fully met, scored 1.0) from **aspirational** or stretch OKRs
  (expected to average around 0.7).

```text
OBJECTIVE: New customers get value on day one
  KR1 Median time-to-first-report falls from 3 days to 4 hours
  KR2 Onboarding completion rises from 42% to 70%
  KR3 Day-7 retention of new accounts rises from 55% to 65%
  (not KRs: "build new onboarding flow", "run 5 interviews";
   those are tasks that serve the KRs)
Type: aspirational. Score at quarter end 0.0-1.0 per KR.
```

| Common OKR failure | Fix |
| :--- | :--- |
| key results are tasks ("launch X") | measure the outcome the task is meant to change |
| too many: 8 objectives × 5 KRs | 1–3 objectives per team per quarter |
| individual OKRs used for performance review | keep OKRs at team level; people sandbag if pay depends on them |
| set and forgotten | review weekly or fortnightly; update confidence |
| all stretch, or all committed | label each; don't punish a 0.7 on a stretch goal |
| business-as-usual work written as OKRs | keep "keep the lights on" as health metrics, not OKRs |
| cascaded top-down only | teams propose; leaders align |

### SMART

From George T. Doran, "There's a S.M.A.R.T. way to write management's goals and objectives" (*Management Review*,
November 1981). His original letters: **Specific, Measurable, Assignable, Realistic, Time-related**. Later versions
swap in Achievable, Relevant and others. SMART suits individual development goals and committed deliverables; it is
a poor fit for exploratory work where the target isn't knowable yet, and Doran himself noted not every objective can
be quantified.

## Performance management

Most performance problems are expectation problems. Before concluding someone is underperforming, check:

| Question | If "no" |
| :--- | :--- |
| Did they know, specifically, what good looks like? | write it down with them |
| Have they had direct, specific feedback that there's a gap? | give it now (SBI) |
| Do they have the skill, tools, information and time? | train, resource, or re-scope |
| Is something outside work going on? | ask, adjust temporarily, point to support |
| Is the role still the right fit? | consider a different role before an exit |

### PIPs done honestly

A **performance improvement plan** is a written, time-boxed set of expectations. Be honest about which kind you are
running:

- **A real improvement plan**: you believe they can succeed; goals are achievable; you'll invest coaching time.
  Say so.
- **A documented exit**: you've already decided. Then a PIP is cruel theatre. A direct conversation and a fair
  severance are usually kinder and cleaner.

```text
PERFORMANCE PLAN (real improvement version)
Role expectation: ______________________________
Gap (specific, with examples and dates): ________
What success looks like by [date] (measurable):
  1. ...
  2. ...
Support I will provide: weekly 30-min check-in;
  pairing with ____; training on ____
Check-ins: [dates]
If the goals are met: plan closes, noted as resolved.
If not met: next step is ____ (be explicit).
Signed/acknowledged: ____  Date: ____
```

### Firing well

- **No surprises**: the person should already know from earlier conversations that their job was at risk.
- Decide first, then have a short conversation; don't negotiate or relitigate in the meeting.
- Do it early in the week, in private, with HR or a witness if needed, and with logistics ready (pay, equipment,
  access, what the team will be told).
- Be generous where you can: severance, a reference for what they did well, time to say goodbye if appropriate.
- Tell the team promptly and briefly, without disparaging the person.
- Employment law varies widely by country and state; get advice before any dismissal.

## Hiring

### What predicts job performance

Schmidt and Hunter's 1998 meta-analysis (*Psychological Bulletin*) put structured interviews, work samples and
general mental ability (GMA) tests at the top. Sackett, Zhang, Berry and Lievens (2022, *Journal of Applied
Psychology*) showed that earlier corrections for range restriction were overdone: most validity estimates fall by
about .10–.20, and **structured interviews became the top-ranked** procedure.

| Method | Schmidt & Hunter 1998 | Sackett et al. 2022 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| structured interview | .51 | **.42** |
| work sample test | .54 | .33 |
| general mental ability test | .51 | .31 |
| unstructured interview | .38 | .19 |

- Values are corrected correlations with job performance. Even .42 leaves most variance unexplained: the 80%
  credibility interval for structured interviews runs from about .18 to .66.
- Structured means: the same job-related questions for every candidate, anchored rating scales, scoring each answer
  independently before discussing, and multiple interviewers.
- Unstructured "chat and gut feel" interviews are half as predictive and let bias in.

### Scorecards and the A method

Geoff Smart and Randy Street's *Who: The A Method for Hiring* (2008) has four steps:

1. **Scorecard**: the role's mission, 3–8 measurable outcomes, and the competencies required.
2. **Source**: build a pipeline, mostly from referrals, before you need it.
3. **Select**: a screening interview, a chronological career interview (their "Who interview"), focused interviews
   on scorecard outcomes, and reference interviews.
4. **Sell**: persuade the candidate you want.

```text
ROLE SCORECARD
Mission (1 sentence): why this role exists
Outcomes (measurable, 12 months):
  1. Ship X so that Y improves from A to B by [date]
  2. ...
Competencies (5-8, each defined):
  - Ownership: fixes problems outside their lane
  - ...
Interview plan: which interviewer probes which outcome
  or competency, with which questions.
Rating: 1-4 per competency, anchored examples for 1 and 4,
  scored before the debrief.
```

| Hiring mistake | Fix |
| :--- | :--- |
| hiring for "culture fit", meaning "like us" | define the values and behaviors; assess those |
| everyone asks the same easy questions | assign each interviewer specific competencies |
| debrief where the most senior voice anchors everyone | written scores submitted before discussion |
| hiring fast to relieve pain | a bad hire costs more than a gap; keep the bar |
| no work sample for a skills role | a short, paid or time-boxed realistic task |

## Meetings

Grove splits meetings into **process-oriented** (regularly scheduled: one-on-ones, staff meetings, operations
reviews) and **mission-oriented** (called to solve a specific problem or make a decision, often once).

| Meeting | Purpose | Cadence | Keys |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| one-on-one | the report's issues, coaching | weekly | their agenda first |
| team or staff meeting | cross-team issues, decisions, context | weekly | agenda sent in advance; decisions recorded |
| standup | coordination and blockers | daily or async | under 15 min; problems taken offline |
| decision meeting | make one decision | as needed | pre-read; named decider; options on paper |
| retro | improve how the team works | per sprint or project | blameless; one or two changes, with owners |
| all-hands | context, direction, recognition | monthly | Q&A, including anonymous questions |

**Written pre-reads.** Amazon does no slide presentations: teams write narratively structured six-page memos, read
silently at the start of the meeting in a kind of "study hall" (Bezos, 2017 shareholder letter). The memo forces
complete thinking; the silent read ensures everyone has actually read it. See
[persuasive writing](/writing/nonfiction/persuasive-writing/) for how to write one.

```text
DECISION MEMO (1-2 pages, sent 24h before)
Decision needed: one sentence. Decider: name. By: date.
Context: what's true now, with numbers.
Options:
  A. ... Pros / cons / cost / risk
  B. ...
  C. Do nothing: what happens
Recommendation and why.
What would change our mind.
Reversibility: one-way door or two-way door?
```

Meeting hygiene: no agenda, no meeting; start with the decision needed; end with decisions, owners and dates;
shortest default length the calendar allows; anyone may leave a meeting they're not contributing to.

## Decision rights: RACI and DACI

Most slow decisions have unclear decision rights, not hard trade-offs.

| RACI | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Responsible** | does the work (can be several) |
| **Accountable** | owns the outcome and signs off; exactly one person |
| **Consulted** | gives input before the decision (two-way) |
| **Informed** | told after (one-way) |

| DACI (Atlassian's version) | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Driver** | runs the process: gathers stakeholders and information, gets a decision by the agreed date |
| **Approver** | the one person who makes the decision |
| **Contributors** | have expertise and a voice, but no vote |
| **Informed** | told once it's made |

RACI suits ongoing responsibilities; DACI suits one-off decisions. The rule that matters in both: **one** accountable
person or approver. Two approvers means nobody is.

## Managing up

John Gabarro and John Kotter's "Managing Your Boss" (*Harvard Business Review*, 1980) argues the boss relationship is
one of mutual dependence that you should manage deliberately. Their advice, paraphrased: understand your boss's
goals, pressures, strengths, weaknesses and preferred working style; understand your own; and build a relationship
that suits both, keeping them informed, being dependable and honest, and using their time selectively.

| Do | Don't |
| :--- | :--- |
| learn how they like information: written or verbal, detail or summary | send the same format to every boss |
| bring problems early, with a recommendation | surprise them, especially in public |
| make their goals your team's priorities, or argue openly to change them | quietly work on a different agenda |
| write a short weekly update: progress, risks, asks | make them chase you for status |
| disagree in private, commit in public | undermine a decision to your team |
| ask what they'd need from you to delegate more | wait to be given autonomy |

```text
WEEKLY UPDATE TO YOUR MANAGER (5 minutes to read)
Headline: on track / at risk / off track, one line why
Done this week: 3 bullets, outcomes not activity
Next week: 3 bullets
Risks: what might slip, what I'm doing about it
Asks: decisions or help I need from you, by when
People: anything about the team you should know
```

## Maker's schedule vs manager's schedule

Paul Graham's essay "Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule" (July 2009): managers work in one-hour slots; makers
(programmers, writers) need half-day blocks at least. "A meeting commonly blows at least half a day, by breaking up a
morning or afternoon." His fix was office hours clustered at the end of the day.

- As a founder-engineer you're on both schedules. Protect maker blocks deliberately (for example, mornings) and batch
  meetings into the rest of the day.
- Give your team the same protection: meeting-free days or blocks, async standups, and your own one-on-ones next to
  their existing meetings rather than in the middle of a clear afternoon.
- The more reports you have, the less maker time you'll get. A common rule of thumb: past about 5–8 reports, stop
  owning critical-path work and keep coding on things nobody is blocked by.

## Burnout and workload

The WHO's ICD-11 describes **burn-out** as an occupational phenomenon (not a medical condition) resulting from
chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, with three dimensions: energy depletion or
exhaustion; increased mental distance from, or cynicism about, one's job; and reduced professional efficacy.

| Signal | What you might see |
| :--- | :--- |
| exhaustion | late-night messages, missed holidays, "just tired", more sick days |
| cynicism | sarcasm about the product or leadership, "what's the point", withdrawal from discussions |
| reduced efficacy | quality slipping in someone who used to be strong, decisions avoided, more errors |
| changes | a talkative person goes quiet, or the reverse; camera off; shorter answers |

Christina Maslach and Michael Leiter's work links burnout to mismatches in six areas of work life: **workload,
control, reward, community, fairness and values**. Only the first is about hours; the others are about how the work
is managed.

What a manager can do:

- Ask directly in one-on-ones: "How sustainable is your load, 1 to 10?" Act on anything under 6.
- Cut scope, not quality or sleep. Say out loud what the team will stop doing.
- Treat heroics as a process failure to fix, not a culture to celebrate.
- Model it: take your holiday, don't send messages at midnight (schedule them).
- Rotate on-call and unglamorous work fairly.
- Know the support available (employee assistance program, health cover) and point people to it; you're not a
  therapist.

## Scripts for hard conversations

Keep them short, specific and kind. Say the headline in the first sentence.

```text
UNDERPERFORMANCE (first direct conversation)
"I want to talk about the last two sprints. Three of your
five tickets slipped, and the payment bug went to
production without tests. That's below what the role
needs, and I'm worried about it. What's going on from
your side?"
[listen]
"Here's what I need to see over the next four weeks: ...
What support would help? Let's check in every Friday."
```

```text
BEHAVIOR THAT AFFECTS THE TEAM
"In Tuesday's design review you called Sam's proposal
'junior-level thinking'. Sam didn't speak for the rest of
the meeting, and two people mentioned it to me. I need
disagreement to be about the idea, not the person. Can you
tell me how you saw it?"
```

```text
SAYING NO TO A PROMOTION (for now)
"I'm not putting you forward this cycle. You're strong on
X and Y. The gap for senior is Z: leading a project across
teams end to end. Here's a project that would show it.
If you deliver it, I expect to put you forward next cycle."
```

```text
LAYING SOMEONE OFF (role eliminated)
"I have difficult news. We're reducing the team and your
role is one of those affected. Today is your last working
day. This is a business decision, not a judgment of your
work. [HR name] will take you through pay, notice and
benefits. I'll gladly be a reference for you."
```

```text
RECEIVING CRITICISM FROM A REPORT
"Thank you for telling me. That can't have been easy.
Can you give me an example so I understand it properly?"
[listen, don't defend]
"I'm going to think about this and try ___. Tell me
if you don't see a difference in a couple of weeks."
```

## First 90 days as a new manager

Michael Watkins' *The First 90 Days* (2003) is the standard book on leadership transitions. A checklist for a
first-time manager of a small team:

```text
DAYS 1-30: LISTEN AND LEARN
[ ] One-on-one with every report: goals, frustrations,
    how they like feedback, what they'd change
[ ] Meet your manager: their expectations, how they'll
    judge success at 90 days and at a year
[ ] Meet key peers and stakeholders: what they need from
    your team, what's not working
[ ] Read the last quarter's docs, metrics, incidents
[ ] Set up weekly one-on-ones and a team meeting
[ ] Don't change processes yet, unless something's on fire

DAYS 31-60: CLARIFY AND DECIDE
[ ] Write a one-page team charter: purpose, priorities,
    how we work, what we won't do
[ ] Agree team goals or OKRs with your manager
[ ] Pick one or two early wins that matter to the team
[ ] Make the delegation levels explicit (delegation board)
[ ] Give your first specific feedback, positive and
    corrective

DAYS 61-90: IMPROVE AND BUILD
[ ] Fix the biggest process pain the team named
[ ] Address any performance issue you've confirmed
[ ] Agree a growth goal with each report
[ ] Ask the team and your manager for feedback on you
[ ] Plan the next quarter
```

## Common new-manager mistakes

Linda Hill's research on new managers (*Becoming a Manager*, 1992; "Becoming the Boss", HBR 2007) found they expect
their authority to come from the position and to control their reports, then discover that authority has to be
earned through credibility and that the job is building the team, not managing individuals one by one.

| Mistake | Better |
| :--- | :--- |
| still acting as the best individual contributor | measure yourself by team output (Grove) |
| taking over when things get hard | coach; delegate at the right level; accept "different" |
| avoiding hard feedback to stay liked | radical candor: care and challenge |
| treating everyone the same way | adjust to task-relevant maturity |
| canceling one-on-ones | protect them; move, don't cancel |
| being a friend first | be friendly, fair and consistent; friendship with reports gets complicated |
| hiding problems from your boss | bad news early, with a plan |
| shielding the team from all context | share the why; people make better decisions with it |
| changing everything in the first month | listen for 30 days first |
| ignoring your own workload and learning | get a mentor or peer group of managers |
