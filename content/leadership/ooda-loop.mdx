John Boyd's observe–orient–decide–act loop: where it came from, what Boyd actually drew (not the four-box cycle on
most slides), each phase with the questions to ask and the ways it fails, tempo and "getting inside the opponent's
loop", and how to use it for competitive response, incidents, product work and sales. Decision tools in general
are in [decision-making](/thinking/mental-models/decision-making/); how intent lets a team run fast loops without
a boss in every one is in [mission command](/leadership/mission-command/); the ownership mindset that goes with
decentralised loops is in [Extreme Ownership](/leadership/extreme-ownership/).

## John Boyd in one table

| Item | Detail |
| :--- | :----- |
| Who | John Richard Boyd (1927–1997), US Air Force fighter pilot, retired as a colonel in 1975 |
| Korea | arrived March 1953, flew 22 missions in the F-86 Sabre before the July 1953 armistice; no kills |
| "40-second Boyd" | instructor at the Fighter Weapons School, Nellis AFB (late 1950s); standing bet that, starting from a position of disadvantage, he could beat any pilot in simulated air combat in under 40 seconds |
| *Aerial Attack Study* | his fighter-tactics manual (1960; revised edition dated 11 August 1964), adopted as Air Force doctrine |
| Energy–maneuverability (E-M) theory | developed in the early 1960s at Eglin AFB with civilian mathematician **Thomas Christie**; two-volume report 1964 |
| Fighter Mafia | with Pierre Sprey, Everest Riccioni and others, pushed the F-15 and the Lightweight Fighter that became the F-16 |
| Later work | from 1976 a series of papers and briefings on conflict, strategy and command, compiled as *A Discourse on Winning and Losing* |
| Influence | US Marine Corps maneuver-warfare doctrine; Dick Cheney (briefed 1981, consulted again in 1990 before Desert Storm) |

**E-M theory** compares aircraft by their ability to gain or shed energy, using specific excess power:

$$
P_s = V \cdot \frac{T - D}{W}
$$

where $V$ is speed, $T$ thrust, $D$ drag and $W$ weight. A positive $P_s$ at a given speed, altitude and g-load
means the jet can climb or accelerate there; comparing $P_s$ plots for two jets shows where each wins a turning
fight. The habit it built (turn a "black art" into measurable trade-offs, then design around them) runs through
everything Boyd did afterwards.

## Where the loop comes from

Boyd never wrote a book. His ideas live in one essay and a set of slide briefings, some of them hours long, that he
revised for two decades.

| Work | Date | What it contributes |
| :--- | :--- | :------------------ |
| *Destruction and Creation* (essay) | 3 September 1976 | how we break old mental concepts apart and build new ones; the epistemology under "orientation" |
| *Patterns of Conflict* (briefing) | grew through the late 1970s–1980s; final version December 1986 | history of war from Sun Tzu to blitzkrieg; "fast transients"; operating inside the adversary's observation–orientation–decision–action loop |
| *Organic Design for Command and Control* | May 1987 | orientation as the centre of command; implicit over explicit; trust; "appreciation and leadership" instead of command and control |
| *The Strategic Game of ? and ?* | June 1987 | strategy as shaping and adapting; the "snowmobile" illustration of creative synthesis |
| *Conceptual Spiral* | August 1992 | the same destruction/creation cycle applied to science, engineering and innovation |
| *The Essence of Winning and Losing* | first edition 28 June 1995, last revision January 1996 | the only diagram of the OODA loop Boyd ever drew |
| *A Discourse on Winning and Losing* | compiled set; Air University Press edition (ed. Grant Hammond) March 2018 | the briefings in one volume |

*Patterns of Conflict* speaks of the adversary's "observation-orientation-decision-action time cycle or loop" and
*Organic Design* of the "O-O-D-A loop", but for about 20 years Boyd described the loop only verbally, usually as a
simple circle. When he finally drew it in 1995 it looked nothing like the four-box cycle people had copied from
his talks (Chet Richards, *Boyd's OODA Loop*, 2020).

## Boyd's loop vs the four-box cycle

<Diagram src="/images/diagrams/leadership-ooda.svg" label="John Boyd's OODA loop sketch from The Essence of Winning and Losing (1995). Four columns: Observe, Orient, Decide, Act. Outside information and unfolding circumstances flow into Observations. Observations feed forward into a large Orient box containing five interconnected elements: cultural traditions, genetic heritage, new information, previous experiences, and analyses and synthesis in the middle. Orient feeds forward to Decision (hypothesis), which feeds forward to Action (test). Two dashed arrows labelled implicit guidance and control run from Orient over the top: one back to Observations and one straight to Action, bypassing Decide. Along the bottom, feedback arrows run from Decision and from Action back to Observations, and a third arrow labelled unfolding interaction with environment runs from Action back to Observations." caption="Boyd's own OODA sketch (after The Essence of Winning and Losing, 1995)" />

| Popular version | Boyd's version |
| :-------------- | :------------- |
| four equal boxes in a circle | Orient is the big box; the other three are small |
| strict sequence: observe, then orient, then decide, then act | many parallel paths; observation, orientation and action run continuously |
| every action passes through an explicit decision | **implicit guidance and control** goes straight from Orient to Act (trained, intuitive response) and to Observe (what you choose to look at) |
| feedback only from the result of the action | feedback from both Decide and Act, plus the unfolding interaction with the environment |
| decision is the choice | decision is a **hypothesis**; action is the **test** of it |
| the aim is to cycle faster | the aim is better orientation than the adversary, so you can shape events and they cannot keep up |

Boyd's note on the sketch: "Note how orientation shapes observation, shapes decision, shapes action, and in turn
is shaped by the feedback and other phenomena coming into our sensing or observing window." In *Organic Design* he
is blunter: "Orientation is the Schwerpunkt." (Schwerpunkt: the German term for the focus of main effort.)

**Implicit guidance and control** is the important arrow. A trained pilot, surgeon or on-call engineer does not
deliberate over most moves: well-built orientation goes straight to action, and the explicit Decide step is for the
cases the repertoire does not cover. Boyd's organisational conclusion is that a team whose members share an
orientation needs fewer explicit orders, which is why he tied the loop to trust and to German mission tactics
(*Auftragstaktik*; see [mission command](/leadership/mission-command/)).

## The four phases in depth

| Phase | What it is | Questions to ask | Failure modes |
| :---- | :--------- | :--------------- | :------------ |
| **Observe** | sensing: data, events, what others say and do, results of your own last action | What changed? What are we not measuring? What would we expect to see if we were wrong? Who is closest to the ground and what are they seeing? | only watching the dashboards you already have; filtering by rank ("the intern's report can wait"); stale data; confusing activity metrics with outcomes |
| **Orient** | making sense: fitting observations to mental models shaped by culture, experience, training and analysis | What story explains all of this, including the anomalies? What would a competitor or customer conclude? Which of our assumptions is oldest? What would have to be true for the opposite view? | confirmation bias; one model for everything; groupthink; "we've always done it this way"; orientation not updated after the world moved |
| **Decide** | choosing a course of action, which Boyd labels a hypothesis | What are two or three real options? What does each assume? Is this reversible? Who owns the call and by when? | analysis paralysis; decision by committee; deciding without saying so; treating the decision as final truth instead of a bet |
| **Act** | doing it, which also tests the hypothesis and changes the environment | Did we act at the speed the situation needs? How will we know if it worked? What signal do we watch next? | slow handoffs; acting without instrumenting the result; no feedback path to Observe; acting and then defending the action instead of learning |

### Observe

- Widen the aperture: customers, frontline staff, logs, competitors, regulators, your own failed experiments.
- Separate **signal you pulled** (you went looking) from **signal pushed at you** (alerts, the loudest voice).
- Orientation decides what gets observed (the implicit-guidance arrow back to Observe). If the team "doesn't see"
  something obvious in hindsight, the fix is usually in Orient, not in more data.

### Orient

Boyd lists five inputs to orientation: **genetic heritage, cultural traditions, previous experiences, new
information**, and **analyses and synthesis** across them. For a team that translates to:

| Boyd's element | Team equivalent | How to improve it |
| :------------- | :-------------- | :---------------- |
| genetic heritage | temperament, cognitive style, risk appetite | build teams with different defaults on purpose |
| cultural traditions | company culture, industry norms, "how we do things" | name the norms; ask which are still true |
| previous experiences | what the team has lived through | post-incident reviews, rotations, customer calls for engineers |
| new information | fresh observations | faster, less filtered reporting |
| analyses and synthesis | taking models apart and recombining them | write the model down, argue with it, borrow from other fields |

### Decide

A decision is the hypothesis that this action, now, will move things the way you expect. Write down the
expectation so the next Observe step can check it. Match the effort to reversibility (two-way doors fast, one-way
doors carefully; see [decision-making](/thinking/mental-models/decision-making/#one-way-and-two-way-doors)).

### Act

Action is the only step that changes the world and the only one that generates new evidence. An action without a
defined check is not a test, just a guess you cannot learn from.

## Orientation, mental models and *Destruction and Creation*

The 1976 essay is Boyd's theory of how orientation changes. Paraphrased:

| Step | Boyd's term | What happens |
| :--- | :---------- | :----------- |
| 1 | **destructive deduction** | break existing concepts into their parts; forget which whole each part belonged to |
| 2 | **creative induction** | find common threads among the loose parts and synthesise a new concept |
| 3 | check | test the new concept for internal consistency and match with observed reality |
| 4 | repeat | any concept refined only inward eventually mismatches a changing world |

He leans on Gödel's incompleteness theorems, Heisenberg's uncertainty principle and the second law of
thermodynamics to argue that a closed system of ideas, "talking to itself", generates rising confusion and
disorder; the cure is to go outside it and build a broader concept. The analogies are loose (Gödel and Heisenberg
say nothing about organisations), but the practical point survives without them: **mental models decay unless you
deliberately break and rebuild them.**

Boyd's illustration in *The Strategic Game of ? and ?* (1987): take the skis from a ski slope, the outboard motor
from a motorboat, the handlebars from a bicycle and the rubber treads from a toy tank, discard the rest, and
recombine the parts into a **snowmobile**. His summary in *Revelation* (1987): winners can build snowmobiles when
facing uncertainty and unpredictable change, and losers cannot.

For practical mental-model work, see [general thinking models](/thinking/mental-models/general-thinking/) and
[cognitive biases](/thinking/mental-models/cognitive-biases/).

## Tempo and getting inside the opponent's loop

*Patterns of Conflict* (1986) states the core claim: "Idea of fast transients suggests that, in order to win, we
should operate at a faster tempo or rhythm than our adversaries—or, better yet, get inside adversary's
observation-orientation-decision-action time cycle or loop."

| Idea | Meaning | Business reading |
| :--- | :------ | :--------------- |
| **fast transients** | the ability to change from one manoeuvre to another quickly, not top speed | switching cost between plans, not raw velocity |
| **operating inside the loop** | your actions arrive faster than the opponent can re-orient, so their picture is always out of date | the competitor is still reacting to your last move when you make the next |
| **ambiguity** | appearing unpredictable, so the opponent cannot form a picture that matches reality | not signalling your roadmap; several credible options open at once |
| **cheng / ch'i** | Sun Tzu's expected and unexpected strokes: fix attention with one, strike with the other | a visible launch that draws the response while the real bet lands elsewhere |
| **goal** | collapse the adversary's system into confusion and disorder, so they over- and under-react | a rival that keeps changing price and messaging without regaining momentum |

Elsewhere in *Patterns* Boyd describes blitz and guerrilla forces operating "in a directed, yet more indistinct,
more irregular, and quicker manner" than their adversaries: quickness is one item on the list, not the whole list.
Being inside someone's loop is about **relative orientation**, not speed alone.

### The F-86 vs MiG-15 story, and what is wrong with it

Boyd's founding example was Korea, 1951–53. The MiG-15 out-climbed the F-86 Sabre and hit harder, yet Sabres won
most fights. His explanation (as recounted in Ian Brown's history and Boyd's biographies): the F-86's **bubble
canopy** gave the pilot a better view (faster Observe), and its **fully hydraulic flight controls** let the pilot
roll from one manoeuvre into the next faster (fast transients). Each exchange, the Sabre pilot re-oriented and
acted sooner, and the MiG pilot fell further behind.

The evidence is weaker than the story:

| Claim | What later research found |
| :---- | :------------------------ |
| roughly 10:1 kill ratio (792 MiGs for 78 Sabres) | the USAF's own post-war review (Sabre Measure Charlie) cut the ratio by about half; a RAND analysis put the ratio at about 1.8:1 overall and about 1.3:1 against Soviet-flown MiGs; Dorr, Lake and Thompson suggest about 2:1 |
| the gap came from the jets' OODA advantages | pilot experience varied hugely (rotating Soviet units, then less-trained Chinese and North Korean pilots); US pilots ambushed MiGs near their bases; Soviet pilots were barred from chasing damaged Sabres over the sea; the Sabre also had a radar-ranging gunsight and g-suits |

The Sabre probably did win, just not by 10:1 and not for one reason. Use the story as an illustration of fast
transients, not as proof that the loop wins wars (see Hankins, *Flying Camelot*, 2021, and Robinson's summary in
the references).

### Speed is not the point

Chet Richards, who worked with Boyd, argues that "faster through the loop" was never Boyd's claim. A deceiver
*wants* the opponent to decide fast on a false picture, and a martial artist may look slower than a flailing
opponent while being inside their loop. Richards on Honda's motorcycle war with Yamaha (from Stalk
& Hout, *Competing Against Time*, 1990): Honda launched 113 new models to Yamaha's 37 in 18 months, but it won
because each launch taught it more about what customers wanted. His conclusion: "Just going through some loop
without learning anything is a waste of time, money, and energy, and going faster just wastes more, more quickly."

Workable rule: **first get the orientation advantage (better picture, surprise, ambiguity); then use tempo to
exploit it.** Tempo without orientation is thrashing.

## Maneuver warfare and the Marine Corps

| Year | Event |
| :--- | :---- |
| 1980 (January) | Boyd briefs *Patterns of Conflict* to Marine officers at Quantico; instructor Michael Wyly revises the tactics curriculum he taught around it |
| early 1980s | civilian analyst William Lind and Marine reformers push maneuver warfare in the *Marine Corps Gazette* |
| 1989 (March) | **FMFM 1 *Warfighting*** published under Commandant Gen. Alfred M. Gray, written by Capt. John Schmitt; maneuver warfare becomes Marine Corps doctrine, with Boyd's ideas as a central pillar |
| 1997 | revised as **MCDP 1 *Warfighting*** under Gen. Charles Krulak; Schmitt credited about three-quarters of the changes to Boyd's direct comments; Boyd died on 9 March 1997, four months before publication |

**Maneuver warfare** in *Warfighting*'s sense aims to shatter the enemy's cohesion with rapid, focused and
unexpected actions, rather than to wear it down by attrition. Its vocabulary (tempo, focus of effort, commander's
intent, decentralised command, surfaces and gaps) maps directly onto business competition and is the bridge from
OODA to [mission command](/leadership/mission-command/). Source for this section: Ian T. Brown, *A New Conception
of War* (Marine Corps University Press, 2018).

## Business applications with worked examples

The loop is most useful where there is an adaptive counterpart (a competitor, an attacker, a failing system, a
buyer) and conditions change faster than annual plans. The scenarios below are illustrative.

### Competitive response

A rival cuts the price of its mid-tier plan by 30%.

| Phase | Weak loop | Strong loop |
| :---- | :-------- | :---------- |
| Observe | sales hears it from one lost deal; CEO sees a tweet | win/loss data for the last 4 weeks; which segments the rival targets; churn and pipeline by segment |
| Orient | "they're coming for us, match it" | they are losing money on that tier (public filings, headcount cuts); our buyers pick on integrations, not price; the cut targets SMBs where we are weakest anyway |
| Decide | match the price across the board | hold price; bundle an integration for mid-market; offer a switching credit to 50 at-risk accounts; review in 3 weeks |
| Act | new pricing page in 2 days; revenue per mid-tier customer down 30% | targeted offer live in 5 days, instrumented by segment |
| Feedback | none: price never goes back up | churn in the at-risk list vs control; win rate against the rival |

The strong loop is not faster to act, but its orientation is better, and it keeps the option to change course.

### Incident response

A production API starts returning 5% errors at 14:02.

```text
OBSERVE  14:02 alert; error rate by endpoint, region, version;
         deploy log shows release 4.12 at 13:55
ORIENT   errors only on /checkout, only on 4.12 pods;
         pattern matches the new payment client (runbook #7)
DECIDE   hypothesis: 4.12 payment client; roll back 4.12
ACT      14:09 rollback started; watch error rate + latency
FEEDBACK 14:14 errors back to baseline -> hypothesis holds;
         open postmortem, keep 4.12 blocked
```

Implicit guidance and control is what the runbook and practice buy you: an experienced on-call engineer goes from
"errors on new pods right after a deploy" straight to "roll back" without a meeting. Incident command structures
exist to protect that loop from too many observers and too many deciders.

### Product iteration

A team ships a new onboarding flow every week. Observe: activation rate, session recordings, support tickets.
Orient: which step loses people, and why (confusing copy, missing data, wrong user). Decide: one change, written as a
hypothesis ("removing the workspace step raises day-1 activation from 41% to 45%"). Act: ship to 50% of new sign-ups.
Feedback: read the result the following week. The weak version ships something every week and never writes down
the expected result, so the team cycles fast and learns nothing.

### Sales

In a discovery call the seller's loop runs in seconds: observe what the buyer says and avoids, orient against
what similar buyers turned out to need, decide the next question or proof point, act, and watch the reaction.
Good sales training builds implicit guidance (experienced reps do not consult a playbook mid-call). Across a deal,
the loop is slower: every meeting is an Act that should produce an observation (who else is involved, what the
real deadline is, what the alternative is).

## OODA vs PDCA vs Build–Measure–Learn

| | OODA | PDCA / PDSA | Build–Measure–Learn |
| :-- | :--- | :---------- | :------------------ |
| Origin | John Boyd, USAF, 1970s–1995 | Walter Shewhart (1930s), popularised by W. Edwards Deming, who preferred Plan–Do–Study–Act | Eric Ries, *The Lean Startup* (2011) |
| Built for | competition against an adaptive opponent under uncertainty | improving a stable process | finding a product and business model under extreme uncertainty |
| Centre of gravity | Orient (mental models) | Check/Study (measured result vs plan) | Learn (validated learning about customers) |
| Pace | continuous, many loops at once, seconds to months | discrete cycles, weeks to months | as fast as the minimum viable product allows |
| Commitment | late: keep options, adapt as the picture changes | early: plan, then run it | small bets: build the smallest thing that tests the riskiest assumption |
| Opponent | explicit | none | none, though competitors matter |
| Best use | competitive moves, incidents, negotiations, crises | manufacturing quality, operations, recurring processes | new products, experiments |
| Typical misuse | "just go faster" | plans that never reach Act, or Check skipped | building without a hypothesis; vanity metrics |

They are complementary: PDCA for the machine, Build–Measure–Learn for the product, OODA for the fight. See
[idea to MVP](/startups/idea-to-mvp/playbook/) for the product loop.

## How to improve your loop

Improving the loop is mostly about orientation and friction, not about working faster.

| Lever | Why it helps | Concrete moves |
| :---- | :----------- | :------------- |
| **Decentralise decisions** | removes the round trip to the boss from every loop | decision rights by reversibility; written intent (see [mission command](/leadership/mission-command/)) |
| **Shared orientation** | people who see the world the same way need fewer words (Boyd's "implicit over explicit") | shared customer calls, incident drills, reading the same data, strategy memos everyone can restate |
| **Fewer handoffs** | each handoff adds queue time and loses context | small cross-functional teams owning a metric end to end |
| **Better sensors** | Observe is only as good as its inputs | outcome metrics, not activity metrics; direct customer contact; alerting on symptoms users feel |
| **Faster feedback** | the loop cannot close until the result is visible | feature flags, staged rollouts, daily numbers, short sales cycles for experiments |
| **Pre-decided responses** | turns Decide into implicit guidance for known situations | runbooks, playbooks, pre-agreed thresholds ("if churn > 3%, pause the campaign") |
| **Deliberate re-orientation** | stops model decay | quarterly assumption review, pre-mortems, red teams, hiring people who disagree |
| **Degrade the opponent's loop** (competitive settings) | the other side of Boyd's idea | ambiguity about your plans, several credible options, moves that force them to re-plan |

### Loop health checklist

```text
[ ] We can name the 3-5 signals that tell us the situation
    changed, and someone watches each one.
[ ] Frontline observations reach decision-makers in hours,
    not at the monthly review.
[ ] Our key assumptions are written down, dated, and were
    last challenged within a quarter.
[ ] Every significant decision records the expected result
    and the date we will check it.
[ ] Routine situations have pre-agreed responses; nobody
    waits for approval to execute them.
[ ] Most decisions are made by the people closest to the
    problem, within a written intent.
[ ] We run a review after incidents, launches and losses,
    and the actions from it get done.
[ ] We know roughly how long our loop takes (signal ->
    decision -> action -> visible result) and it is
    shorter than the situation's rate of change.
```

## Templates

### OODA situation card

```text
SITUATION: ____________________   DATE: ________
OBSERVE
  What changed (facts, sources, times):
  What we are not seeing / cannot measure:
ORIENT
  Our current explanation:
  Alternative explanations (at least 2):
  Assumptions this rests on (oldest first):
  What would change our mind:
DECIDE
  Options considered:
  Chosen action (as a hypothesis):
    "If we ___, we expect ___ by ___."
  Reversible? Y/N   Owner: ______   Deadline: ______
ACT
  First step, who, when:
  Signal we will watch and when we check it:
FEEDBACK (fill in later)
  What happened vs expected:
  What we now believe that we did not before:
```

### Orientation review (quarterly, 60 minutes)

```text
1. List the 10 beliefs our strategy depends on
   (customers, competitors, technology, us).
2. For each: evidence for, evidence against, date last
   tested.
3. Pick the 3 weakest. For each, design the cheapest
   test that could prove it wrong this quarter.
4. Ask: what would a smart competitor conclude from the
   same data? What would a new hire find odd about us?
5. Record changes to our picture; share them with every
   team that makes decisions on these beliefs.
```

## Criticisms and misuses

| Criticism | Substance | What to do about it |
| :-------- | :-------- | :------------------ |
| **Speed for its own sake** | the popular "cycle faster than the enemy" reading ignores deception, learning and the cost of rework; lean product development found that delaying some decisions made better cars faster | optimise for orientation and learning per loop; use tempo to exploit an advantage you already have |
| **It is not really a loop** | British officer Jim Storr (quoted by Osinga): "Military forces do not in practice wait to observe until they have acted"; observation, orientation and action are continuous | use Boyd's full sketch, not the circle; think of parallel loops at different levels |
| **Too vague to test** | historian Michael Hankins argues the model is loose enough that supporters and critics each see what they want in it; it is one of many descriptions of intuitive decision-making | treat it as a checklist and a vocabulary, not a scientific theory |
| **Shaky founding evidence** | the Korean War kill ratio behind the F-86 story was heavily inflated | do not cite the 10:1 figure; cite the idea |
| **Slogan use** | "get inside their OODA loop" in a slide deck, with no change to decision rights, sensors or feedback | if nothing about who decides, what is observed or how fast results return has changed, nothing has changed |
| **Assumes an adversary** | many business problems are not duels; a loop tuned to beat a rival can ignore customers | pair with customer-facing loops (Build–Measure–Learn) |
| **Encourages centralisation when misread** | a leader who thinks they must personally run the loop becomes the bottleneck | Boyd's own conclusion was the opposite: shared orientation and trust, so many people run loops within intent |

The loop is at its best as a shared vocabulary ("our orientation is stale", "that's implicit guidance, no meeting
needed", "we acted, but where's the feedback?") and as a reminder that the scarce resource is an accurate picture of
a changing situation.
