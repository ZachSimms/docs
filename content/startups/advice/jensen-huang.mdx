What Jensen Huang, Nvidia's co-founder and only CEO, has actually said and done: the near-death bets (Sega, the
RIVA 128, CUDA), "zero-billion-dollar markets", a flat organisation with about 60 direct reports and no one-on-ones,
"speed of light" planning, and the "pain and suffering" line, each checked against transcripts and dated
reporting. The reasoning method has its own sheet, [first principles](/thinking/first-principles/); conventional
management practice is on [management](/leadership/management/); the build pipeline is
[idea to MVP](/startups/idea-to-mvp/playbook/). Compare with [Elon Musk](/startups/advice/elon-musk/),
[Steve Jobs](/startups/advice/steve-jobs/) and the shorter entries on [other founders](/startups/advice/founders/).

**Why listen:** he has run the same company for 33 years, through at least two near-bankruptcies and a decade in
which its main strategic bet (CUDA) cost margin with no market to show for it, and the bet then became the
foundation of the AI industry. Few founders have explained their operating system in as much detail, on the record.
**Why be careful:** the story is told backwards from the most spectacular timing win in tech history. Much of it
worked because deep learning happened to need exactly what Nvidia had built, and some of his habits (public
criticism, 60 direct reports, seven-day weeks) are documented as hard on the people around him.

Quotation note: Huang says most things many times in slightly different words, and articles polish them. Quotes
below are checked against a transcript (Stanford GSB, Stripe, Acquired, NTU) or a named reliable report; numbers
he gives from memory (direct reports, his lowest market cap) drift between tellings, and that is flagged.

### Sega and the RIVA 128: admit it, then bet with simulation

At National Taiwan University's commencement (27 May 2023) Huang told the Sega story as a lesson in humility: if
Nvidia finished the console it would have built "inferior technology", incompatible with Windows; if it quit, it
would run out of money. "Either way, we would be out of business." He told Sega's CEO the approach was wrong and
still asked to be paid. "Confronting our mistake, and with humility asking for help, saved Nvidia."

The RIVA 128 is the companion lesson. On Acquired (October 2023) he explained that the normal chip cycle (tape
out, find bugs, tape out again) would have killed them, so they bought an emulator from a company that was shutting
down, wrote and tested the whole driver stack on it, and went straight to production. His summary for founders:
betting the farm means taking "all the risky things" from the future and pulling them in in advance; "everything in
the future that we can simulate today, we prefetch it." The point is not courage. It is doing the verification
before the bet, not after.

### Thirty days from going out of business

Stephen Witt's *New Yorker* profile (November 2023) reports that after the RIVA 128, "for years to come he opened
staff presentations with the words 'Our company is thirty days from going out of business.'" and calls it the
unofficial corporate motto. Huang said on *The Joe Rogan Experience* in December 2025 that he has used the phrase
"for 33 years", and that he is "always in a state of anxiety" (as reported by *Fortune*). Read it as a deliberate
bias against complacency, and note the cost he admits alongside it.

### CUDA: a decade with no market

Huang's own account at Stripe Sessions (April 2024): CUDA added "a ton of cost into our chips", there were no
applications, customers would not pay a premium, so "your gross margins get crushed". Asked whether it was a smash
hit: "It was an incredible disaster overnight." Asked how long it took to work: "Probably 10 years."

| What he did | Why | Source |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| put CUDA on every GeForce, not just a pro line | gamers paid for the chips and built the installed base developers needed | NTU 2023 |
| kept every chip architecturally compatible | "That's the only unnegotiable rule in our company. Everything else is negotiable." | Acquired 2023 |
| founded GTC and courted scientists one domain at a time | seismic, CT, molecular dynamics came before AI | NTU 2023 |
| tracked early indicators, not results | see [zero-billion-dollar markets](#zero-billion-dollar-markets) | Stanford GSB 2024 |
| argued the board through it in words | "They had to believe the reasoning, the words," not a spreadsheet | Stripe 2024 |

### The GPU as a category, and the data centre as the computer

Nvidia's marketing head Dan Vivoli coined "graphics processing unit" for the 1999 GeForce: "We invented the
category so we could be the leader in it" (*New Yorker*). Twenty years later Huang used the same move at a larger
scale. On Acquired he said he bought Mellanox because "if you want to be a data center company, building the
processing chip isn't the way to do it"; what defines a data centre is networking, and AI training spreads one job
across many processors, the inverse of commodity cloud workloads. He called it "one of the best strategic decisions
I've ever made".

## Zero-billion-dollar markets

The phrase is genuinely his and documented in several places. On Acquired (2023) he called it "our way of saying
there's no market yet, but we believe there will be one". At Stripe Sessions (2024): "I'd rather be a market maker,
market creator than a market taker," and "a zero-billion-dollar market is a good way to cause the company to think
about how to go create something for the first time." The *New Yorker* describes it as exploratory products "which
not only do not have competitors but don't even have obvious customers".

| Principle | What it means | Source | Apply it by… |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **go where there's no consumption** | position before the market forms, so few competitors are shaped for it when it arrives | Acquired 2023 | asking what job people currently don't do at all because it's too hard or costly |
| **importance over market size** | "Is this important work? And if we didn't do it, would it happen without us?" If others will do it, let them | Stanford GSB 2024 | dropping ideas that a competent competitor will obviously build anyway |
| **early indicators of future success** | KPIs like gross margin are results; look for the earliest evidence you're on the right road, and "decouple the result from evidence that you're doing the right thing" | Stanford GSB 2024 | defining 2–3 leading signals (repeat usage, unsolicited referrals) before revenue exists |
| **reasoning over spreadsheets** | "I hate spreadsheets because you can make spreadsheets do whatever you want… I love words… Words are reasoning." | Stripe 2024 | writing the case for a bet as an argument, then attacking it |
| **hard is a moat** | hard, long projects deter "less committed" rivals | Stripe 2024 | preferring the idea with a schlep in it to the weekend-clone idea |
| **retreat to create** | leaving phones freed Nvidia to build "a zero billion dollar robotics market" | NTU 2023 | killing a crowded side-line to fund the thing only you can do |

**The catch:** a zero-billion-dollar market is indistinguishable from a market that will never exist until years
later. Nvidia could afford a decade of waiting because graphics paid the bills. A startup without a cash-generating
core can't; for you, the lesson is the *early indicators*, not the decade. Test demand with the methods in
[validation](/startups/idea-to-mvp/validation/) before you bet the runway on a market that isn't there.

## The management system

Huang describes Nvidia's organisation as something designed from first principles for what it builds. At Stanford
GSB (March 2024): "don't worry about how other companies' org charts look… Remember what an organization is designed
to do." On Acquired he said Nvidia is wired "much more like a computing stack" than a military chain of command.

| Principle | What it means | Source | Apply it by… |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **about 60 direct reports** | "NVIDIA's leadership team is 60 people… they all report to me"; the people reporting to a CEO need the least management; he estimates it removes "something like 7" layers | Stripe Sessions, April 2024 | keeping a small team flat and resisting a middle layer until it clearly pays for itself |
| **no one-on-ones** | "I don't do one-on-ones… Almost everything that I say, I say to everybody all at the same time." He "really discourages" them for his leaders too | Stripe 2024 | discussing decisions in the team channel or meeting, not in DMs |
| **feedback in public** | "I give you feedback right there in front of everybody… feedback is learning. For what reason are you the only person who should learn this?" | Stripe 2024 | reviewing work in the open, reasoning aloud, and being corrected in the open yourself |
| **information isn't power** | he rejects cultures where "the information that you possess is the reason why you have power"; leaders earn their job by reasoning and helping others | Stanford GSB 2024; Acquired 2023 | sharing the numbers, the runway and the problems with everyone |
| **Top 5 Things (T5T)** | anyone can email the few most important things on their mind; he reads them daily to "detect the weak signals" | *New Yorker* 2023; Tae Kim, *The Nvidia Way* (2024), via *Fortune* | a weekly five-bullet note from each person, read by the founders |
| **no periodic plan** | "there is no five-year plan, there is no one-year plan, there is no plan — there is just what we are doing"; plan continuously | Sana *Strange Loop* interview, 2023 (quoted by *Tom's Hardware*) | a rolling priority list reviewed weekly instead of an annual plan |
| **mission is the boss** | "there's a phrase in the company called 'mission is the boss'"; a mission is concrete ("Build Hopper"), and people and resources are wired to it across the org | Acquired 2023 | naming one owner and one concrete mission per project, whatever the org chart says |
| **no reporting meetings** | "No reporting meetings… just problem meetings"; CEOs are "pinch hitters" for things "nobody else can or nobody else is" doing | Stripe 2024 | replacing status meetings with written updates; meeting only on problems and ideas |
| **failure must be shared** | managers of a failed graphics card presented every decision behind it to a few hundred colleagues | *New Yorker* 2023 | blameless post-mortems presented to the whole team |
| **no task beneath you** | "I used to clean toilets… you can't show me a task that's beneath me" | Stanford GSB 2024 | founders doing support, QA and sales calls themselves |
| **torture into greatness** | he rarely fires; "I'd rather improve you than give up on you… I'd rather torture you into greatness" (tongue in cheek, he says) | Stripe 2024 | coaching hard before replacing, with a stated bar |
| **tenure for craft** | "you can achieve operational excellence through process but craft can only be achieved with tenure" (Collison quoting him backstage; Huang agreed and expanded) | Stripe 2024 | keeping a stable core team; treating churn as lost skill |

### What it costs

Huang is candid that the design has a price. On Acquired: "The downside is the pressure on the leaders is fairly
high," because in his system nobody is powerful merely by being closer to information. At Stripe he said 60 reports
is not "scalable downward": lower levels "need more and more supervision". Conventional advice treats one-on-ones as
the core of coaching and career development (see [one-on-ones](/leadership/management/#one-on-ones)); Huang's
system assumes senior, self-directed people who want neither.

## Speed of light and first principles

| Principle | What it means | Source | Apply it by… |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **speed of light** | when scheduling, estimate the absolute fastest a task could conceivably be done (the physical limit), then work backwards to an achievable plan; the benchmark is physics, not your competitors or last year | *New Yorker* 2023 (Witt's description) | asking "what would this take if nothing waited on anything?", then listing every wait that remains |
| **first principles** | learn how something is done, then ask "given the conditions today… given the instruments, the tools… how would I redo this? How would I reinvent this whole thing?" | Stanford GSB 2024 | redesigning a process from today's tools rather than patching last year's |
| **how hard can it be?** | "it's probably just a textbook away. You're probably one archive paper away" | Stanford GSB 2024; Acquired 2023 | giving yourself a weekend with the manual before hiring the expert |
| **check what changed** | after an 80% share-price fall: "Did physics change? Did gravity change?… if none of those things changed, you change nothing, keep on going" | Stanford GSB 2024 | writing down the assumptions behind a bet so you can tell noise from evidence |
| **prefetch the future** | pull every risky thing forward and simulate it before committing | Acquired 2023 | prototyping the riskiest part first; see [riskiest assumption testing](/startups/idea-to-mvp/validation/#riskiest-assumption-testing) |
| **work backwards from the end** | "I go forward in time and look backwards" | Stanford GSB 2024 | writing the launch announcement before the spec |

The "speed of light" idea is the most transferable thing on this page. Competitor benchmarks tell you how slow
everyone is; the physical limit tells you how much of your plan is waiting, hand-offs and habit. In software the
"physics" is small: compile, deploy and test times. Most of a two-month estimate is usually queueing, which is what
[fixed time, variable scope](/startups/idea-to-mvp/playbook/#fixed-time-variable-scope) attacks.

The Stanford GSB write-up of his View From The Top interview is titled "Jensen Huang on How to Use First-Principles
Thinking to Drive Decisions" (published 25 April 2024; the talk itself was on 1 March 2024).

## Pain, suffering and resilience

### What he said, and where

The line was **not** said at the GSB interview, although it is often placed there (Patrick Collison did so on stage
at Stripe). Stanford's Institute for Economic Policy Research reports it from Huang's keynote Q&A with John Shoven
at the **2024 SIEPR Economic Summit, 1 March 2024** (the same day as his GSB View From The Top). Asked for advice for
Stanford students: "For all of you Stanford students, I wish upon you ample doses of pain and suffering." *Fortune*
(13 March 2024) quotes the reasoning around it: "People with very high expectations have very low resilience—and
unfortunately, resilience matters in success"; "One of my great advantages is that I have very low expectations";
"Greatness comes from character and character isn't formed out of smart people—it's formed out of people who
suffered."

<YouTube id="cEg8cOx7UZk" title="Keynote by NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang at 2024 SIEPR Economic Summit" channel="Stanford Institute for Economic Policy Research (SIEPR)" />

At Stripe Sessions (April 2024) he explained it: people connect passion with happiness, but "when you want to build
something great it's not easy to do… I don't love every day of my job… but I love the company every single second."
Hence "I wish upon you greatness, which by my way of saying it, I wish upon you plenty of pain and suffering."

| Source | Line | Take it as |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| SIEPR Summit, 1 March 2024 | "I wish upon you ample doses of pain and suffering" | resilience is built, not taught |
| Stripe Sessions, April 2024 | "You have to suffer. You have to struggle… in order to really appreciate what you've done" | don't pick work by how pleasant each day is |
| Acquired, October 2023 | "I wouldn't do it… building Nvidia turned out to have been a million times harder than I expected… Nobody in their right mind would do it" | the honest counterweight: he would not start again |
| Acquired, October 2023 | "that's the superpower of an entrepreneur. They don't know how hard it is… To this day, I trick my brain into thinking, how hard can it be?" | naivety is a feature at the start |
| *New Yorker*, 2023 | on Oneida: "Back then, you just had to toughen up and move on"; "I find that I think best when I'm under adversity… My heart rate actually goes down" | his resilience story is autobiographical |
| NTU commencement, 2023 | the CUDA years "forged our corporate character to endure the pain and suffering that is always needed to realize a vision" | used for companies, not just people |

The Acquired answer is the one to remember. Asked what he would start if he were 30 again in 2023, he said he
wouldn't: if the founders had known "the pain and suffering, just how vulnerable you're going to feel… the
embarrassment and the shame", nobody would start a company. The same person who wishes suffering on students also
says the suffering would have stopped him. The support that got him through, he said, was "family, friends", and
colleagues and early investors who "not one time gave up".

## Critiques and limits

| Issue | What's documented |
| :--- | :--- |
| **survivorship and timing** | Nvidia nearly died in 1996–97 and, by Huang's account, was valued at a few billion dollars or less for years after CUDA. The AI payoff arrived because academic researchers chose GPUs (AlexNet, 2012), a turn Huang did not plan; he calls these moments "a diving catch" and says "You want to position yourself near the tree". The dozens of 1990s graphics start-ups that made equally bold bets are forgotten. |
| **60 direct reports doesn't transfer** | Huang himself says it is not "scalable downward". It works for a founder-CEO with 30 years of context, very senior staff and near-total authority; most managers lack all three, and the conventional norm of regular one-on-ones exists because most people need coaching and a private channel. |
| **public feedback can humiliate** | the *New Yorker* reports he can "lose his temper" when people fudge answers; Huang: "When the mismatch is great, then it comes out as anger." A former colleague: "I've been afraid of Jensen sometimes." Public correction teaches the room only where safety is high; elsewhere people stop speaking. |
| **pain-and-suffering romanticism** | the line is advice to elite students with a safety net. His own evidence cuts both ways: he says he wouldn't start again, works seven days a week and is "always in a state of anxiety" (Rogan, December 2025). Suffering is a cost that sometimes comes with great work, not a method for producing it. |
| **customer concentration** | in fiscal 2026 one direct customer was 22% of revenue and another 14% (Nvidia 10-K); the large cloud companies are also designing their own AI chips |
| **supply concentration** | Nvidia designs but does not fabricate; its wafers come from foundries such as TSMC and Samsung, and the 10-K flags dependence on supply "from Taiwan and South Korea" |
| **export controls and China** | US controls on advanced chips to China began in October 2022; in April 2025 a licence became required for the H20, leading to a \$4.5bn charge; US officials later said they expected 15% or more of licensed-sales revenue (10-K). China (by customer headquarters) fell from \$25.0bn (19%) of revenue in fiscal 2025 to \$19.7bn (9%) in fiscal 2026. In September 2025 China's internet regulator told major firms to stop buying Nvidia's China-specific RTX Pro 6000D. |
| **antitrust** | FTC suit to block the Arm deal (December 2021; abandoned February 2022); French competition authority search of Nvidia's offices (September 2023); US Justice Department investigation opened June 2024; China's SAMR opened a probe in December 2024 and on 15 September 2025 said a preliminary finding showed Nvidia broke its anti-monopoly law over conditions attached to the Mellanox deal |
| **his numbers drift** | direct reports go from 40 to 50 to 60 across a year of interviews; his low-point market cap is "just above \$1 billion" at NTU and "a couple of \$2–\$3 billion" on Acquired. Quote the source, not the meme. |

**Blunt conclusion:** the strategic habits (admit the mistake, simulate before betting, track early indicators,
benchmark against the physical limit, keep one architecture compatible for decades) are sound and cost nothing to
copy. The organisational ones (60 reports, no one-on-ones, public critique) are tuned to one founder with unusual
authority and an unusually senior team. The pain-and-suffering rhetoric describes his path; it is not evidence that
suffering causes success.

## What transfers to a small startup

| Transfers | Doesn't transfer |
| :--- | :--- |
| telling a customer or partner early that your approach is wrong | a decade in a zero-billion-dollar market without a cash-generating core |
| verifying the risky part before the big commitment (prefetch the future) | betting the company on one tape-out without the emulator |
| early indicators of future success when revenue is too early to judge | ignoring results indefinitely because you "believe" |
| "speed of light" estimates that expose waiting and hand-offs | 60 direct reports once you have managers who are new to managing |
| everyone hears decisions and reasoning at the same time | public criticism without trust built first |
| a weekly five-bullet note from everyone | reading 100 of them a day instead of doing the work |
| one concrete mission per project as the "boss" | "no plan" as an excuse for no priorities |
| strategic retreat from a crowded side-market | romanticising suffering for yourself or your team |

## Takeaways checklist

```text
[ ] I told someone this week about a mistake in our
    approach, and asked for help if we need it
[ ] The riskiest part of the next big commitment was
    tested or simulated before we committed
[ ] Each bet has 2-3 early indicators we check weekly
[ ] I estimated the "speed of light" for our next
    milestone and listed every wait in the plan
[ ] We asked "would this happen without us?" before
    starting a new project
[ ] Decisions and their reasoning are shared with the
    whole team at the same time
[ ] Everyone sends a five-bullet weekly note; I read
    them looking for weak signals
[ ] Each project has one concrete mission and one owner
[ ] Our priorities are reviewed continuously, not
    once a year
[ ] Post-mortems are presented to everyone, without
    blame
[ ] I cut or retreated from one crowded side-market
[ ] Nobody, including me, is running on anxiety alone
```
