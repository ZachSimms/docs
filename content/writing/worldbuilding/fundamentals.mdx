Method and principles for inventing a world that serves a story or a game: how much to build, which direction to
build in, how to keep it consistent, how to reveal it without stopping the story, and how to keep the notes usable.
The follow-on sheets go deeper on the [physical world](/writing/worldbuilding/physical-world/),
[societies and cultures](/writing/worldbuilding/societies-cultures/),
[magic and technology](/writing/worldbuilding/magic-technology/) and [game worlds](/writing/worldbuilding/game-worlds/).
Plot, character and prose craft live in [story structure](/writing/fiction/story-structure/),
[character](/writing/fiction/character/) and [scene and prose](/writing/fiction/scene-prose/). All example worlds on this
sheet are invented for illustration.

## Why build a world at all

A world exists to **generate and constrain** stories or play. Everything else is a hobby (a fine one, but a
different activity).

| Job the world does | In fiction | In games |
| :----------------- | :--------- | :------- |
| Creates **conflict** | scarce resources, rival factions, taboos that characters break | goals, obstacles, enemies, faction quests |
| Creates **constraints** | what characters cannot do forces cleverer choices | rules the player learns and exploits |
| Carries **theme** | the setting argues something the plot then tests | mechanics and spaces express the idea |
| Supplies **texture** | specific, surprising detail makes scenes feel lived-in | environmental art, props, barks, item text |
| Sets **expectations** | genre signals tell the reader what kind of story this is | tone, rules, difficulty, what counts as success |
| Produces **surprise** | consequences the reader didn't see coming but accepts | emergent situations the designer didn't script |

**The iceberg principle**: show a fraction, know more. The reader or player sees perhaps a tenth of what you have
decided; the rest shows as confidence. A character who glances at a shrine without explanation reads as someone who
lives there. The "tenth" is a rule of thumb, not a measurement, and the principle has a limit: the hidden part only
helps if it changes what is on the page (how people talk, what they fear, what they assume).

<Note kind="tip">Test every piece of lore with one question: does this change a decision a character or player makes? If
not, it can wait in the notes.</Note>

## Top-down vs bottom-up

Two directions of work. Most working writers and designers alternate: sketch the big frame lightly, build up from
scenes, then go back and fix the frame.

| | **Top-down** (outside-in) | **Bottom-up** (inside-out) |
| :- | :------------------------ | :------------------------- |
| Order | cosmos → planet → continents → climates → nations → cities → people | scene or character → street → city → region → only as far as needed |
| Strengths | global consistency (rivers, climates, trade make sense); good for maps, strategy games, shared settings, tabletop campaigns | every detail earns its place; fast to first draft; texture is specific because it is seen through a person |
| Weaknesses | months of notes before a single scene; details nobody will see; "encyclopaedia voice" leaks into prose | contradictions appear later (a desert beside a rainforest with no reason); the edges of the map are blank |
| Typical failure | the world is finished and the story is not started | the world is a stage set that collapses when the story travels |
| Best for | epic fantasy, 4X and grand strategy games, open worlds, shared-world projects | short stories, character-driven novels, linear games, first drafts |
| Mitigation | set a deadline; build only to the scale of the first story | keep a rules ledger; do one top-down pass after the draft |

A practical hybrid, **the zoom method**: write one page at planet scale (climate belts, a continent shape, two or three
great powers), then build bottom-up from the opening scene, and zoom out whenever the story crosses a boundary you
haven't decided.

## Two traditions: sub-creation and its critics

The craft argument about worldbuilding is old and unresolved. Both sides are useful.

### Tolkien: sub-creation and the Secondary World

J. R. R. Tolkien's essay **"On Fairy-Stories"** (given as the Andrew Lang lecture at St Andrews on 8 March 1939,
published in *Essays Presented to Charles Williams*, 1947) calls the fantasy author a **sub-creator** who makes a
**Secondary World**. He writes that the successful story-maker "makes a Secondary World which your mind can enter", and
that "the moment disbelief arises, the spell is broken". The art that makes this work is achieving "the inner
consistency of reality". Tolkien contrasts this **secondary belief** with Coleridge's "willing suspension of disbelief":
the reader should not have to try.

Practical reading: the more a world differs from ours, the harder consistency is to achieve, and the more it matters.

### Le Guin: the world is made of words

Ursula K. Le Guin's **"From Elfland to Poughkeepsie"** (a 1972 talk, published as a Pendragon Press booklet in 1973 and
collected in *The Language of the Night*, 1979) argues that in fantasy "the act of speech is the act of creation" and
"every word counts". Her target is fantasy written in an ordinary, borrowed style, so that the invented land turns out
to be the reader's own everyday world in disguise: "Poughkeepsie" rather than "Elfland" (paraphrased). The
lesson for worldbuilders: a world is experienced **through prose style**, diction and names, not through the notes.

### M. John Harrison: "the great clomping foot of nerdism"

In a blog post titled **"Very Afraid"** (his blog *Uncle Zip's Window*, 27 January 2007), M. John Harrison wrote that
"every moment of a science fiction story must represent the triumph of writing over worldbuilding". He called
worldbuilding dull, said it "literalises the urge to invent" and "numbs the reader's ability to fulfil their part of the
bargain", and concluded: "It is the great clomping foot of nerdism. It is the attempt to exhaustively survey a place
that isn't there."

His argument, stated fairly:

- Fiction works by **suggestion**; readers co-create the world from fragments. Exhaustive description takes that job
  away from them.
- Worldbuilding can become a **substitute for writing**: the author feels productive while no story is being told.
- A world that is "complete" in the notes pushes authors to **show the notes**, which produces infodumps.
- Harrison is himself a skilled worldbuilder (his Viriconium stories deliberately change the city from book to book),
  so the target is the encyclopaedic habit, not invention.

### Reconciling them

| Claim | Tolkien side | Harrison side | Working synthesis |
| :---- | :----------- | :------------ | :---------------- |
| Consistency matters | essential to belief | less than the reader's imagination | consistent **rules**, suggestive **details** |
| How much to build | enough for inner coherence | as little as the writing needs | build for decisions, not for completeness |
| Where the world lives | in the Secondary World | in the prose and the reader | in the prose, backed by notes nobody sees |
| Gaps | a flaw to minimise | a feature that invites the reader in | leave deliberate gaps; know why they are there |

Games shift the balance towards Tolkien: a player can walk to the edge of the map and poke at the rules, so systems must
hold under pressure. Even so, the game only needs consistency where the player can reach.

## Consistency and the rules ledger

Readers forgive strangeness; they do not forgive **contradiction**. Keep a **rules ledger**: a short, numbered list of
every hard fact the story has committed to, with where it was established.

| Ledger field | Example (invented world) |
| :----------- | :----------------------- |
| ID | R-014 |
| Rule | Salt-glass shatters if carried across running water |
| Scope | physical law; applies everywhere |
| First stated | Ch. 3, the ferry scene |
| Consequences | glass merchants use bridges only; river cities import no salt-glass |
| Exceptions | frozen rivers (untested in text) |
| Status | canon / tentative / retired |

Rules of consistency:

- **Hard vs soft facts.** Hard facts are on the page and cannot change without a revision. Soft facts are in the notes
  and can still move. Mark which is which.
- **Characters can be wrong.** A character stating a "fact" commits the character, not the world. Unreliable in-world
  sources are a feature (see [societies and cultures](/writing/worldbuilding/societies-cultures/) on history).
- **Exceptions need reasons.** An exception without a cause reads as an error. An exception with a cause is a plot
  hook.
- **Scale checks.** Numbers drift: travel times, army sizes, city populations, dates. Put them in the ledger and do the
  arithmetic once (see the travel table in [physical world](/writing/worldbuilding/physical-world/)).
- **Continuity pass.** Before each draft is finished, search the manuscript for every ledger rule and check it.

## The ripple method

The most productive single technique: take one change and ask **"if this exists, what else must be true?"**, then ask
it again of each answer, **three levels deep**. First-order effects are obvious; the second and third orders are where
the original, story-generating material is. The idea that good SF predicts not the car but the traffic jam is often
credited to Frederik Pohl, but Quote Investigator traces its core to Isaac Asimov in 1953 ("It is easy to predict an
automobile in 1880; it is very hard to predict a traffic problem"); Pohl wrote a version in 1968 and disclaimed credit
for it.

### Worked example: the city where the dead keep for a year

Invented premise: in the city of Carrow, a corpse does not decay for one year and a day, then crumbles to dust within an
hour.

| Level | Question | Answers |
| :---- | :------- | :------ |
| 1 | What must be true immediately? | Bodies must be **stored** somewhere for a year. Funerals happen twice: at death and at crumbling. Everyone knows exactly when a body will crumble. |
| 2 | What follows from storage? | **Year-houses** on the city edge; a trade of **wardens**; storage fees that the poor can't pay; theft of well-preserved bodies. |
| 2 | What follows from two funerals? | The first is private grief, the second a public release; debts are settled at the second. |
| 2 | What follows from the known date? | **Inheritance waits a year**; wills can be contested while the body "remains"; a legal status between alive and dead. |
| 3 | Consequences of the in-between status? | The **Grey Year**: the dead still own property and hold office; a murdered magistrate still "rules" for a year, so a faction can kill a judge to freeze a court. |
| 3 | Consequences of body theft? | Anatomists in Carrow are centuries ahead of neighbours because fresh corpses are plentiful; medicine is a Carrow export; foreign students come, and some smuggle bodies out. |
| 3 | Consequences of fees? | Paupers' bodies are pooled in a common house; a burial guild runs a mutual-insurance scheme that has quietly become the city's largest bank. |

Three lines of premise produced a legal thriller hook (the frozen court), an economic power (the burial-guild bank), a
reason for foreigners to visit (medicine) and a crime (body smuggling). None of it needs to be explained on the page;
it only needs to be **true** so that scenes can show it.

<Callout type="info" title="Ripple categories to sweep">
  For each change, ask about: food and water, work and money, family and marriage, law and crime, war, religion and
  death, status and fashion, language and slang, architecture, travel, children and education, and what the powerful do
  with it first.
</Callout>

## The novum and theme-driven worldbuilding

### The novum

Darko Suvin's *Metamorphoses of Science Fiction* (1979) defines SF as a literature of **cognitive estrangement** and
names the **novum** (Latin, "new thing"): the novelty or innovation that makes the story's world differ from the
reader's, validated by logic (cognition) rather than by pure wonder. Useful as a discipline beyond SF:

- Identify your **one to three nova**. Everything else should behave as normal until the ripple method says otherwise.
- **Hold everything else constant.** Stacking unrelated novelties (psychic cats, FTL, dragon cults, time travel) dilutes
  each one and multiplies the consistency load.
- The novum is where the **story questions** come from. Suvin argues that significant SF is a roundabout way of
  commenting on the author's own society.

### Building from theme

Decide what the story is **about** (not the plot: the question underneath it), then build the world to put pressure on
that question.

| Theme question | World built to test it (invented examples) |
| :------------- | :----------------------------------------- |
| Is memory identity? | memories can be sold; the poor sell childhoods to pay rent |
| Does power corrupt, or reveal? | a hereditary gift of command that only works on people who love the holder |
| What do we owe the dead? | the city of Carrow above: the dead hold property for a year |
| Can a society outgrow violence? | a treaty-magic that kills anyone who breaks an oath, used to enforce peace |

Theme-driven worlds avoid the "kitchen sink" problem (the Turkey City Lexicon's term for a story crammed with every idea
the author had). Every major feature should either sharpen the theme or be cut.

## Exposition: infodumps vs integrated detail

**Infodump** (the Turkey City Lexicon also calls it an "expository lump"): the story stops so the author can explain.
Occasionally fine (a short, well-voiced dump can be a pleasure; Jo Walton notes that Neal Stephenson has raised it to an
art form), usually a drag.

**Incluing**: Jo Walton's term, in "SF reading protocols" (Tor.com, 2010), for "scattering pieces of information
seamlessly through the text to add up to a big picture". The reader assembles the world like a detective.

| Technique | How it works | Example (invented) |
| :-------- | :----------- | :----------------- |
| **Character need** | explain only what a character needs now, in their terms | she counts the ferry fare twice: salt-glass in her pack means taking the long bridge |
| **Conflict exposition** | two characters disagree about a fact, so the fact comes out as argument | "They'll freeze the court." "Only if the judge is dead a full year." |
| **Assumed knowledge** | characters treat the strange as ordinary; no gloss | "Pay the warden, then come to supper." |
| **Contrast with an outsider** | a newcomer asks, but only what an outsider would plausibly ask | a foreign student asks why the bodies don't smell |
| **Objects and documents** | signs, ledgers, letters, song lyrics, item text | a year-house receipt with the crumbling date stamped on it |
| **Delayed explanation** | show the effect first, explain later (or never) | a funeral with no body, then a second with dust |
| **Consequence over cause** | show what the rule does, not how it works | a smuggler caught with a perfectly preserved grandmother |

Antipatterns:

- **"As you know, Bob"**: characters tell each other things both already know, for the reader's benefit (a named
  entry in the Turkey City Lexicon). Fix: give one of them a reason not to know, or cut it.
- **The encyclopaedia opening**: a prologue of history and geography before any character wants anything.
- **The glossary crutch**: invented terms that only make sense with the appendix open.
- **Lecturing through a mentor**: a wise elder explaining the magic system in a single unbroken speech.

<Note kind="tip">Rule of thumb for drafts: no more than a short paragraph of explanation at a time, and never before the
reader cares about the character who needs it.</Note>

## Names and naming

Names are the most-read worldbuilding on the page. They carry culture, history and tone before any description.

| Principle | Practice |
| :-------- | :------- |
| **One language, one sound** | each culture gets a small sound inventory and syllable pattern (see [societies and cultures](/writing/worldbuilding/societies-cultures/)); names from the same place should sound related |
| **Distinguishable** | vary first letters, length and rhythm across major characters; avoid Tarn, Tarl and Tarin in one book |
| **Pronounceable enough** | readers subvocalise; if they stumble every time, they will start skimming |
| **Apostrophes and diacritics** | use only if they mean something (a glottal stop, a clan marker) and use them consistently |
| **Meaningful but not punny** | a translated place name (Saltmarch, the Grey Year) conveys meaning; an obvious pun breaks tone |
| **Layered history** | old places keep old names; conquerors rename; ordinary people use nicknames |
| **Real-world echoes** | names signal a real culture whether you mean them to or not; decide deliberately |
| **Don't rename the ordinary** | the Turkey City Lexicon's "call a rabbit a smeerp": a new name for a normal thing adds nothing |

Keep a **name register** (name, meaning, language, first use) in the bible to prevent accidental duplicates.

## The setting bible

A **setting bible** (or world wiki) is the reference you consult while writing. It is a tool for the author, not a
product for the reader.

| Component | Contents |
| :-------- | :------- |
| Pitch | one paragraph: what makes this world different, the nova, the theme |
| Rules ledger | numbered hard facts with source and status |
| Timeline | dated events, including "what the characters believe happened" vs "what happened" |
| Places | one entry per location that appears; map links |
| Peoples and factions | culture sheets and faction sheets |
| Characters | name, faction, goals, first appearance (character craft is in [character](/writing/fiction/character/)) |
| Name register | every coined word and name |
| Open questions | things deliberately undecided, and the date you must decide them by |
| Style sheet | spellings, capitalisation, calendar terms, units, forms of address |

Tools (examples only):

| Tool | Good for | Watch out for |
| :--- | :------- | :------------ |
| Plain Markdown files in folders | portable, searchable, works with version control | links are manual |
| Obsidian | local Markdown with backlinks and a graph view | the graph can become the hobby |
| World Anvil | hosted worldbuilding wiki with templates, maps, timelines | lock-in; template fields tempt you to fill everything |
| Spreadsheet | timelines, travel times, populations, name registers | poor for prose |

<Callout type="warn" title="Bible bloat">
  A bible that takes longer to search than to rewrite is dead weight. Prune entries nobody has used for two drafts.
</Callout>

## Worldbuilding questionnaires

Questionnaires are prompts, not homework. The best known is **Patricia C. Wrede's Fantasy Worldbuilding Questions**
(copyright 1996), hosted by the Science Fiction and Fantasy Writers Association. Wrede is explicit that nobody needs to
answer every question; the point is to provoke thinking about how the setting hangs together. Its sections:

| Section | Covers (summarised) |
| :------ | :------------------ |
| The World | basics; alternate-Earth vs wholly invented worlds |
| Physical and Historical Features | climate and geography, natural resources, world and national history |
| Magic and Magicians | rules of magic, wizards, magic and technology |
| Peoples and Customs | customs, eating, greetings, gestures, language, ethics, religion, population |
| Social Organization | government, politics, crime and law, foreign relations, war, weapons |
| Commerce, Trade, and Public Life | business, transport and communication, science, medicine, arts, architecture, urban and rural life |
| Daily Life | fashion, manners, diet, education, calendar |

How to use one well:

1. Answer only the questions the **current story** touches.
2. For each answer, run **one ripple** ("so what?").
3. Flag questions whose answer would **surprise you** as story material.
4. Skip whole sections that don't matter (a one-city heist novel needs little on foreign relations).

## Scope ladder

How much to decide depends on the form. Decide **depth where the story goes**, and a sketch of everything adjacent.

| Form | Must decide | Can stay vague | Typical bible |
| :--- | :---------- | :------------- | :------------ |
| Short story | the novum, one location in depth, the rules the ending depends on | geography beyond the horizon, history before living memory | a page |
| Novel | 3–6 locations, the main factions and their goals, rules of any magic or tech used in the climax, a timeline of the last generation | distant lands, deep history, economics beyond what drives the plot | 10–40 pages |
| Series | a map, the major powers, the history behind the central conflict, consistent naming systems, the magic's limits | detail of places not yet visited (but leave room for them) | a wiki; a continuity editor helps |
| Tabletop campaign | the start region in depth, factions with goals that move without players, a few rumours per location | anything the players haven't reached; improvise, then record | session notes plus a living wiki |
| Linear game | every space the player enters, the lore the player can read, the rules of every mechanic | everything off the critical path | level docs, lore bible, barks list |
| Open-world or systemic game | biomes, factions, economies and rules at a level the simulation can run; every rule the player can test | handcrafted history beyond what is surfaced in lore | design docs, data tables, a lore bible, tooling |

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Symptom | Fix |
| :------ | :------ | :-- |
| **Monocultures** | the "planet of hats" (TV Tropes' name): every member of a people is the same (the warrior race, the merchant race) | give each culture internal disagreement, classes, regions, dissidents, generations |
| **Medieval stasis** | thousands of years of history with no change in technology, language, borders or dynasties | compress timelines, or explain stasis (a magic, a law, an empire that suppresses change) and make it a story question |
| **Economics that don't work** | huge cities with no farmland; gold everywhere with no inflation; armies that never eat | trace food, water and money for each major city (see [societies and cultures](/writing/worldbuilding/societies-cultures/)) |
| **Everything explained** | no mysteries; the reader never wonders | leave deliberate gaps and contradictions in in-world sources |
| **Earth with the serial numbers filed off** | "Not-England" with swords and elves; the world is Poughkeepsie in costume | change at least one deep assumption and follow it through |
| **Kitchen sink** | too many nova, each underdeveloped | keep one to three; cut or merge the rest |
| **Geography by vibes** | rivers that split, deserts beside rainforests, mountains in random lines | see [physical world](/writing/worldbuilding/physical-world/) |
| **Costless magic** | power solves every problem; stakes vanish | see [magic and technology](/writing/worldbuilding/magic-technology/) |
| **Worldbuilding as procrastination** | a 200-page bible and no chapter one | cap building time; write the first scene now |
| **Infodump prologues** | page one is history | start with a character wanting something |
| **Tourist protagonists** | the hero is a stranger only so things can be explained | use insiders; let the reader catch up |

## Checklist

```text
FOUNDATIONS
[ ] One-paragraph pitch: the world's difference and its theme
[ ] 1-3 nova named; everything else held constant
[ ] Build direction chosen (top-down, bottom-up, zoom)
[ ] Scope matched to the form (short story / novel / game)

CONSISTENCY
[ ] Rules ledger started; every on-page rule recorded
[ ] Ripple method run 3 levels deep for each novum
[ ] Travel times, populations, dates checked by arithmetic
[ ] Exceptions each have a cause

PAGE
[ ] No explanation before the reader cares
[ ] No "as you know, Bob" dialogue
[ ] Invented terms readable without a glossary
[ ] Names follow each culture's sound rules
[ ] Style sheet: spellings, capitals, calendar, units

WORLD
[ ] Each culture has internal difference
[ ] History shows change over time
[ ] Food, water and money traced for key places
[ ] Deliberate gaps and unreliable sources exist
```
